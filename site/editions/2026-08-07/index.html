The AI Read the Chart. The Chart Was Still Incomplete.

Three studies show why model accuracy is only the beginning of validation for AI-derived real-world evidence

August 7, 2026 — Friday edition

A language model can extract a clinical fact almost perfectly and still leave a study with an evidence problem. The missing piece may be in the record, the prompt, or the operating point chosen for review.

Three peer-reviewed studies published within the past month put numbers around those limits. In rheumatoid arthritis, GPT-4o accurately recovered a disease-activity measure when it was documented, but usable evidence appeared for only a fraction of the relevant cohort. In lung cancer, the clinician's prompt, language, and target variable changed the result as much as the model family. In anti-amyloid safety surveillance, an LLM-assisted review caught more events but generated more false positives.

The common lesson is not that language models failed. It is that an RWE pipeline has more than one denominator. Accuracy among documented cases, coverage across the intended cohort, consistency of the clinical specification, and the cost of missed versus excess signals all need their own validation.

The extraction score was 0.97. The evidence coverage was 15.4%.

Reid Weisberg, Chunhua Weng and colleagues tested zero-shot GPT-4o extraction of the Clinical Disease Activity Index, or CDAI, from rheumatoid-arthritis notes. CDAI is useful for longitudinal RWE because it describes disease activity more directly than diagnosis codes or medication history alone.

The researchers identified 2,756 rheumatoid-arthritis patients with notes in a clinical data warehouse covering more than 4.5 million people. The cohort included 1,038 seropositive patients. Among that group, CDAI or its components were extractable for 160 patients — 15.4%.

When the evidence was present, the model performed strongly: precision, recall, and F1 were all 0.97. But that number answers a conditional question — can the model recognize documented CDAI evidence? It does not answer how representative the 160 observable patients are, or whether missing disease-activity documentation differs by severity, care setting, clinician, or time.

The paper is a single-system study, and its abstract does not establish portability across sites or note styles. Still, it exposes a reporting discipline that matters now: every AI-derived RWE variable needs an extraction metric and a coverage metric. Without both, a near-perfect F1 can conceal a narrow window into the intended population.

The prompt carried clinical expertise — and changed the result

Federica Corso and colleagues evaluated four locally deployable small language models on treatment-relevant variables in 897 people with non-small-cell lung cancer. The APOLLO real-world dataset supplied 711 patients in an immunotherapy development cohort and 186 in a tyrosine-kinase-inhibitor validation cohort.

The models were not judged on one generic chart-reading score. The researchers tested zero-shot, few-shot, and clinically annotated prompts across variables including performance status, smoking, and tumor stage. Average F1 differed sharply across models, but the target field and prompt design were just as consequential.

Clinically annotated prompting pushed ECOG performance-status extraction to 0.97 accuracy. Tumor T-stage remained difficult: LLaMA accuracy stayed between 0.39 and 0.48 across prompting strategies. Native-language prompting also mattered. For smoking status, LLaMA's zero-shot accuracy rose from 48% in English to 72% in Italian; with few-shot prompting, it rose from 59% to 82%.

Twenty-six clinicians took part, from medical students to experienced oncologists, and prompt strategy changed agreement patterns between experience groups. The result reframes prompt engineering in RWE: it is not just a technical convenience. It is a place where clinical definitions, local language, and expert assumptions enter the study instrument.

The evidence remains bounded to one cancer setting, a small clinician group, and limited external validation. Implicit staging language was persistently hard. A model that performs well on one structured-sounding variable cannot be assumed to recover another field whose meaning is scattered across narrative text.

Safety surveillance chose sensitivity over perfect precision

Amanda Fong and colleagues evaluated an LLM-augmented pharmacovigilance workflow for anti-amyloid therapies. The retrospective study covered 20,123 clinical documents from 46 patients who received at least one dose in a large mid-Atlantic health system. Adjudication found an adverse event in 35 patients.

The AI-assisted workflow found every adjudicated event, reaching 100% sensitivity with an 89.7% positive predictive value. Standard expert review delivered the inverse tradeoff: 88.6% sensitivity and 100% positive predictive value.

That is not a simple human-versus-model contest. It is a choice about error costs. A safety program may accept extra false positives to reduce missed adverse events, provided the review burden is visible and manageable. Another task may choose differently.

The study is preliminary, retrospective, single-system evidence from only 46 patients. Before operational use, the workflow needs testing across sites, therapies, documentation systems, and lower-prevalence settings. It also needs a workload measure: how many additional records must a reviewer adjudicate for each extra event found?

Friday synthesis: three ceilings on AI-derived evidence

This week's papers did not converge on a winning model. They converged on the limits that determine whether AI output can become credible evidence.

ObservabilityThe record may omit the clinical fact, or a data mapping may lose it before the model ever sees it.
SpecificationPrompts, clinical rules, and endpoint implementations can change a derived variable after source data are available.
OversightVersioned workflows and human adjudication remain necessary where errors have unequal consequences.

Wednesday's edition showed medication evidence disappearing through mapping and note availability. Thursday's edition showed endpoint scores changing when the same extracted inputs were expressed through different specifications. Today's studies add documentation coverage, clinician-shaped prompting, and an explicit sensitivity-precision tradeoff.

The credible pattern is not autonomous evidence generation. It is an auditable chain: disclose what the source data can support, validate each variable in its actual task, freeze the logic that transforms it, and keep a review path for errors whose costs are asymmetric.
