## Ranked evidence

### 1. Expert-Guided g-computation with Large Language Models for Estimating Causal Effects on Timings

- Direct source: [arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.10339) and [complete experimental HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.10339)
- Submission date: August 11, 2026
- Stable ID: `arxiv:2608.10339`
- Content version: `v1`
- Authors: Patrick Vossler, Jialin Ouyang, F. Richard Guo, Anran Huang, Ali Shojaie, Lucas Zier, Fan Xia, and Jean Feng; UCSF, University of Michigan, and University of Washington
- Internal score: 10/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. A GPT-5-assisted pipeline extracts patient-specific causal DAGs from 2,193 deidentified inpatient timelines and combines them with g-computation to estimate the effect of eleven proposed hospital quality-improvement interventions on length of stay.
- What the primary source establishes:
  - The method, called expert-guided g-computation or egg-computation, maps clinical-event timelines into causal Gantt charts. Expert or LLM reasoning supplies intervention relationships that cannot be identified from historical data; statistical timing models supply data-supported conditional distributions.
  - The real-world study used 2,193 adult inpatient timelines from the five most common diagnoses at Zuckerberg San Francisco General Hospital. Timelines combined clinical notes, orders and events, and laboratory or test results.
  - The pipeline generated 1,531 patient-specific DAGs across eleven candidate interventions. The reported LLM API cost was $490.
  - Five hospital QI experts reviewed 24 intervention-hospitalization pairs. Intervention-target specificity was 96.5% for both reviewed interventions, recall was 88.9% and 95.7%, edge precision was 91.8% and 94.2%, and edge recall was 80.2% and 86.0%. Nineteen of 24 LLM and expert-corrected DAG pairs yielded identical time-saved estimates.
  - For 4,790 downstream event timings across 736 hospitalizations, GPT-5 in-context learning achieved a mean absolute error of 24.12 hours, compared with 27.23 for the closest pair-type-median baseline and 28.30 for a zero-gap baseline.
  - Clinicians reviewed intervention-level summaries and patient-level observed-versus-counterfactual timelines. They considered the causal ranking more plausible than ranking interventions by eligibility prevalence alone and highlighted patient-level counterfactual Gantt charts as especially useful.
- Why it matters: this is a concrete architecture for joining unstructured RWD, expert knowledge, and formal causal estimands when an intervention is genuinely new and positivity cannot be satisfied from prior exposure. It makes the LLM a scalable elicitation and structuring layer rather than the final causal authority.
- Evidence limits: this is a preprint and a single-hospital QI study. Only 24 intervention-hospitalization pairs received expert DAG review. Estimated effects assume no cross-patient resource interference, which can make them an upper bound when shared capacity is constrained. Formal uncertainty intervals for expert-supplied inputs remain future work. The analysis supports prioritization, not proof of realized impact after implementation.

### 2. Do AI chatbots find what experts would?

- Direct sources: [arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13786), [experimental HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.13786), and [public analysis repository](https://github.com/QingfangLiu/llm-evidence-retrieval-bias)
- Submission date: August 13, 2026
- Stable ID: `arxiv:2608.13786`
- Content version: `v1`
- Authors: Qingfang Liu, Qiao Jin, Joe D. Menke, Thorsten Kahnt, and Zhiyong Lu; NIH and University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign
- Internal score: 9/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. Three web-enabled frontier chatbots retrieve primary clinical studies for 20 current Cochrane intervention questions, and their outputs are benchmarked against expert included and excluded study sets, a direct evidence-synthesis task.
- What the primary source establishes:
  - Claude Sonnet 5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and ChatGPT GPT-5.5 were prompted as patients, clinicians, and evidence-synthesis researchers, with four independent repetitions across 20 Cochrane intervention reviews: 720 responses in total.
  - Across all responses, mean recall of Cochrane-included studies was 39.2% with a 29.8% standard deviation. Mean retrieval of Cochrane-excluded studies was 5.0% with a 9.4% standard deviation.
  - ChatGPT recorded mean recall of 63.1%, compared with 37.0% for Claude and 17.3% for Gemini. Researcher-role prompts produced 42.8% recall, compared with 38.6% for clinician prompts and 36.1% for patient prompts.
  - Controlling for publication year, citations per year, and open-access status, study sample size was the only independently significant retrieval predictor: odds ratio 1.80 per one-unit increase in log sample size, 95% CI 1.37-2.36.
  - The public repository contains unchanged model answers, review-specific analyses, curated artifacts, figures, and a demo. Licensed Cochrane source packages are not redistributed, so full regeneration requires an authorized local copy.
- Why it matters: evidence retrieval can now be measured as a scientific selection process, not just a citation-hallucination problem. The benchmark exposes model and prompt effects while offering a reproducible pattern for evaluating retrieval systems against expert-curated evidence sets.
- Evidence limits: this is a preprint using general-purpose consumer chatbots in July 2026, not dedicated systematic-review agents. Cochrane inclusion is a strong benchmark but not an absolute measure of every relevant study. The design evaluates recall and selection tendencies, not downstream meta-analytic conclusions. Model versions and web indexes will change.

### 3. How Compliant is Sepsis Treatment? An Expert-Guided Neuro-symbolic Pipeline

- Direct source: [arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13617) and [complete experimental HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.13617)
- Submission date: August 12, 2026; accepted to NeSy 2026 according to the artifact
- Stable ID: `arxiv:2608.13617`
- Content version: `v1`
- Authors: Himanshu Tripathi, Kaushik Roy, Subash Neupane, and Shahram Rahimi
- Internal score: 8/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. A locally executed MedGemma model normalizes medication and microbiology strings from 2,438 MIMIC-IV sepsis episodes, after which expert-defined fuzzy rules generate graded protocol-compliance evidence from longitudinal RWD.
- What the primary source establishes:
  - The cohort contained 2,000 patients and 2,438 sepsis episodes sampled from MIMIC-IV v3.1. Seven standardized files retained medication, microbiology, laboratory, vital-sign, and profile information with minute-level timestamps.
  - MedGemma-4b-it was restricted to semantic normalization. Regex rules verified explicit drug classifications, embeddings checked 213 non-other model classifications with 98.59% confirmation, and remaining edge cases were escalated for expert review. The model did not make compliance decisions.
  - A deterministic Sugeno fuzzy-inference layer encoded eight Surviving Sepsis Campaign rules. The design replaced hard timing cutoffs with graded membership functions set through domain-expert consultation.
  - Under the study's uniform one-hour operationalization, antibiotic timing had the lowest mean score at 0.24 and 13% of episodes met the threshold. Overall mean compliance was 36.7% and median compliance was 37.5%.
  - Low-compliance episodes had a median ICU stay of 5.1 days compared with 3.8 days in the high-compliance group. The authors describe this as an association; the reported correlation across 2,362 episodes was weak at -0.026.
- Why it matters: the pipeline assigns the language model the task it is best suited to—resolving messy clinical vocabulary—then routes safety-critical scoring through transparent expert rules. That separation is a reusable RWD pattern for protocol-adherence analysis.
- Evidence limits: this is a preprint and single public critical-care dataset. The onset anchor can penalize antibiotics administered before formal ICU onset; the study uses a uniform one-hour antibiotic rule that does not stratify by shock status. Missing and undocumented care are conservatively counted as noncompliance. Fuzzy boundaries depend on expert settings, mortality was not evaluated, and length-of-stay comparisons are descriptive rather than causal.

## What changed

The strongest new signal is architectural. Language models are being bounded inside evidence workflows: structuring patient-specific causal graphs for explicit estimands, retrieving studies against expert reference sets, and normalizing messy RWD before transparent rules evaluate care. The next frontier is external validation and uncertainty propagation, not a return to opaque end-to-end answers.
