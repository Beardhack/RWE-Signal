# Research notes - 2026-08-12

## Editorial line

**Headline:** The AI cut estimated review time. It also missed 49% of the search.

Three previously unreported peer-reviewed studies inside the discovery window draw the same boundary across health-economic review, pharmacovigilance, and oncology phenotyping. AI produced meaningful scale and plausible labor savings on structured tasks, but performance weakened where the work required complete retrieval, interpretation of modeling choices, or reconstruction of nuanced safety narratives.

## Ranked evidence

### 1. Bloudek et al. - AI-assisted economic-evidence review saved estimated time but retrieved only half the PubMed set

- Direct source: [PharmacoEconomics](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40273-026-01648-7)
- Accessible record: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42525208/)
- Publication date: July 29, 2026
- Stable ID: `doi:10.1007/s40273-026-01648-7`
- PMID: `42525208`
- Content version: `version-of-record`
- Internal score: 9/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. The validation study tests machine-learning and LLM components across search, screening, and extraction in a systematic review of health-economic evaluations, a core HEOR evidence-synthesis workflow.
- What the accessible primary abstract establishes:
  - The team replicated a previously completed manual systematic review of economic evaluations for chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps.
  - The workflow used the Nested Knowledge platform's machine-learning Robot Screener and LLM-based Smart Screener and benchmarked performance against the human review.
  - The AI-generated search retrieved 22 of 43 PubMed articles in the original review, or 51%.
  - Accuracy exceeded 95% at title-and-abstract screening but fell below 80% at full-text screening.
  - Extraction was reliable for high-level model descriptors and general study characteristics.
  - It was less reliable for model structures, health states, outcomes, sensitivity-versus-scenario analyses, and assumptions involving duration of response, discontinuation, surgery, and mortality.
  - Estimated time savings ranged from about 20% for extraction to 60% for searches and title-and-abstract screening, depending on human validation requirements.
- Why it matters: missing nearly half of the benchmark PubMed set is not an ordinary efficiency tradeoff for a review intended to characterize an evidence base. The study separates useful prioritization and clerical assistance from the completeness and interpretive work required for a defensible health-economic review.
- Commercial context: three authors were affiliated with Nested Knowledge, whose platform was evaluated; the other two were affiliated with Curta. The study's negative and task-specific findings remain informative, but independent replication would strengthen confidence.
- Limits: one prior review in one disease area; accessible abstract only because publisher full text required a subscription; exact denominators for screening and extraction metrics were not available in the accessible record; time savings were estimates and varied with human review requirements.
- Follow-up: independently validate on multiple economic-review corpora, report recall as the governing search metric, disclose task-level denominators and error types, and test whether missed studies or misread assumptions change a model or reimbursement conclusion.

### 2. Warner et al. - pharmacovigilance extraction ranged from near failure to perfect performance

- Direct source: [Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics](https://doi.org/10.1002/cpt.70409)
- Accessible record: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42522449/)
- Publication date: July 29, 2026
- Stable ID: `doi:10.1002/cpt.70409`
- PMID: `42522449`
- Content version: `version-of-record`
- Internal score: 8/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. GPT-4o extracts decision-relevant elements from real individual case safety report narratives in five historical pharmacovigilance signal assessments, with human verification retained.
- What the accessible primary abstract establishes:
  - The retrospective feasibility study used a proprietary intelligent-automation platform and GPT-4o to extract risk factors and responses to dechallenge and rechallenge from individual case safety report narratives.
  - Five historical signal assessments supplied the test cases.
  - F1 scores for risk-factor extraction ranged from 0.444 to 1.000.
  - F1 scores for dechallenge and rechallenge ranged from 0.429 to 0.909.
  - The operating design was machine first and human verified; potential time savings remained after accounting for verification.
  - The abstract does not report the exact case count, task-level prevalence, or quantitative time-saving estimate.
- Why it matters: signal assessment is an evidence workflow in which the omitted or misread detail can alter causal interpretation. The very wide F1 ranges show why average automation claims are inadequate; validation must be element- and signal-specific.
- Commercial context: all authors were affiliated with Eli Lilly, and the evaluated platform was proprietary.
- Limits: retrospective feasibility study; five historical assessments; incomplete task denominators in the accessible abstract; publisher full text required a subscription; proprietary platform; no prospective deployment or independent external validation reported in the accessible record.
- Follow-up: report performance by case element and signal, measure reviewer disagreement and verification burden, evaluate false-negative consequences, and externally test the workflow before reducing human review.

### 3. Abhyankar et al. - a small labeled set supported 2.5 million oncology staging records, within one system

- Direct source: [JCO Clinical Cancer Informatics](https://ascopubs.org/doi/abs/10.1200/CCI-25-00388)
- Accessible record: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42441924/)
- Publication date: July 13, 2026
- Stable ID: `doi:10.1200/cci-25-00388`
- PMID: `42441924`
- Content version: `version-of-record`
- Internal score: 8/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. A fine-tuned LLM extracts real-world cancer-stage variables from routine clinical documentation at patient scale for downstream oncology data use.
- What the accessible primary abstract establishes:
  - The ground-truth sample contained 700 notes from patients with bladder, cervical, colorectal, breast, or prostate cancer in structured data at one integrated health system.
  - For bladder, cervical, and colorectal cancers, 450 notes were divided equally across training, validation, and test sets; another 150 supported targeted error-pattern training.
  - The remaining 100 notes were split between breast and prostate test sets, without additional training for those cancers.
  - The fine-tuned model extracted overall stage; T, N, and M; timeframe; and associated cancer diagnosis.
  - It generated more than 2.5 million staging records for 217,768 patients from more than two million notes.
  - Relation-level precision across six attributes ranged from 0.77 to 1.0 for the first three cancers and from 0.83 to 1.0 for breast and prostate without additional training.
  - The authors were evaluating the model on other solid tumors at three additional health systems; those results were not part of this report.
- Why it matters: the study demonstrates how a modest adjudicated corpus can unlock a large real-world oncology resource. It does not yet establish transportability: the millions of generated records came from the same integrated system that supplied the labeled notes.
- Commercial context: all authors were affiliated with Truveta, and the evaluated model was TLM-Oncology.
- Limits: one health system; only 700 ground-truth notes; publisher full text required a subscription; relation-level precision alone does not describe recall, calibration, patient-level consistency, subgroup performance, or error propagation into downstream studies; external validation was still underway.
- Follow-up: publish multi-system performance, report recall and patient-level temporal consistency, quantify errors by cancer and attribute, and test whether staging misclassification changes cohort definitions or treatment-effect estimates.

## What changed

The evidence is converging across distinct RWE tasks. AI can reduce the work of ranking, extracting, and structuring information, but the failure modes concentrate where evidence decisions are most consequential: exhaustive retrieval, interpretation of model assumptions, causal reading of safety narratives, and transport of phenotypes across sites. Evaluation should therefore follow the downstream decision, not stop at an aggregate accuracy or time-saving number.
