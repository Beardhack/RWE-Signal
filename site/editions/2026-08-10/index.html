The AI Found 3,460 Contradictions. It Could Not Say How Many Were Real.

A two-stage LLM scanned 3,000 discharge summaries and found possible inconsistencies in 69.7% of admissions, but no clinician-labeled corpus existed to measure precision or sensitivity

August 10, 2026 — Daily edition

An AI can make the hidden uncertainty in a health record visible. That does not make the uncertainty resolved.

A new formative study applied two frontier language models to real discharge summaries and returned 3,460 pairs of statements that might conflict. The candidates ranged from laterality errors and medication discrepancies to diagnoses that changed during a hospital stay. Expert review confirmed that some were meaningful. It also showed how easily a legitimate clinical change can resemble a contradiction when time and context are missing.

The study's most important number is therefore the one it could not report. Without a fully adjudicated reference set, the authors could not calculate the pipeline's precision or sensitivity. A flag rate of 69.7% is a workload estimate, not an accuracy result and not a prevalence estimate for erroneous charts.

A separate randomized crossover trial of LLM-assisted evidence review reached a parallel boundary. Reviewers finished an average of 7.9 minutes faster after adjustment, but the confidence interval crossed zero. Both studies show AI producing useful intermediate artifacts before the evidence is strong enough to automate the final judgment.

The detector produced a queue, not a verdict

Jian Lu, Panyu Chen, Chunhua Weng and colleagues reported the EHR-inconsistency pipeline in a July 24 preprint. Gemini 2.5 Pro first searched for conflicting statements inside each note. Gemini 2.5 Flash then performed a cheaper, context-grounded verification pass.

The researchers ran that design across 3,000 randomly sampled MIMIC-IV-Note discharge summaries. The second stage left 3,460 candidate inconsistencies, with at least one candidate in 69.7% of admissions. The output covered demographics, allergies, procedures, diagnoses, laboratory and imaging findings, medications, and discharge planning.

Several reviewed candidates illustrated genuine hazards: one section could document a procedure on the left while another named the right; an allergy could conflict with medication use; a numeric value could change across sections. These are precisely the defects that can contaminate a phenotype or create incompatible variables when notes are reused for research.

Other flags were reconcilable. Admission and discharge medications are expected to differ after treatment. An early suspected diagnosis may appropriately disappear once tests return. Outpatient prescribing conventions can make an incomplete medication list look contradictory. The verification model did not consistently reconstruct those timelines and workflows.

The authors respond by proposing a graded ontology rather than a binary label: strict contradictions at one end, ambiguities that admit multiple readings at the other. Their schema also records where the statements occur, the clinical domain, the encounter phase, and the logical axis—presence, status, value, timing, or laterality.

That framework is a useful precursor to validation. It is not validation itself. Only a subset of candidates received expert review, and the preprint explicitly reserves quantitative performance claims for a future clinician-labeled corpus. Until that exists, the system is best understood as high-recall case finding for human data-quality review, not an autonomous chart-cleaning layer.

The evidence-review trial saved time on average. It did not prove the saving.

A peer-reviewed randomized crossover trial led by Masahiro Ishikane, published July 23 in Open Forum Infectious Diseases, tested a narrower evidence task. Five experienced reviewers extracted predefined information from mpox-related papers with or without an LLM-generated structured summary.

The system used OpenAI's o3 model once per paper, processing the first 10,000 characters under a strict JSON schema. The summaries were stored before the participant sessions. The LLM did not search for studies, screen them, assess the evidence, or propose the clinical action.

The point estimate favored the LLM by about 23%, but the 95% confidence interval ranged from 1.5 minutes slower to 17.3 minutes faster. The trial therefore did not demonstrate superiority on its primary outcome. Accuracy was 100% in both conditions, but that comparison covered only nine LLM-assisted and eleven manual task evaluations, with intended meaning judged by one infectious-disease specialist.

The small trial still improves the evidence conversation because it measures researcher time in a controlled design instead of reporting model accuracy alone. It also exposes the gap between a helpful component and an automated review: PDF downloading remained manual, only data extraction was tested, run-to-run stability was not assessed, and searching and screening remained outside the system.

What changed: candidate generation is ahead of adjudication

Today's new signal is not that language models failed. Both systems produced plausible value: one found chart defects worth reviewing, and one shortened review time on average without an observed accuracy loss.

The change is a clearer measurement boundary. Candidate volume is not precision. A favorable point estimate is not a demonstrated effect. Perfect accuracy across twenty tasks is not evidence of general reliability. These distinctions matter most when an AI artifact becomes an input to a cohort, endpoint, or rapid public-health decision.

The next useful studies are already implied by the gaps: fully adjudicate the contradiction corpus, report precision and sensitivity by error type, expand the randomized review trial across reviewers and domains, and test the entire evidence pipeline rather than a pre-generated summary. Until then, the credible deployment pattern is triage plus audit, not silent correction or autonomous synthesis.
