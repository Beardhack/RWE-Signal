A Sleep Co-Scientist Put 50 Terabytes of Physiology to Work

Specialist agents linked code to five discoveries across roughly 124,000 recordings, while new local and retrieval systems advanced pharmacovigilance and cohort preparation.

RWE-Signal  ·  August 15, 2026  ·  Daily edition

Agentic AI is moving into the machinery of real-world evidence. The most consequential systems now emerging do more than answer questions: they work inside governed data environments, assemble reproducible analyses, and leave artifacts that experts can inspect.

The clearest new example is AI Sleep Co-Scientist, a preprint system that coordinated specialist agents across four clinical and epidemiological cohorts. Two additional projects carry the same architecture into clinical-note pharmacovigilance and longitudinal cohort preparation. Together, they point toward AI that expands the amount of evidence a research team can responsibly interrogate.

Three agents, four cohorts, five studies

Published in a revised preprint on July 29, AI Sleep Co-Scientist connects a Hypothesis Agent, a Preprocessing Agent, and an Execution Agent to a shared, read-only data substrate. The platform spans the Stanford Sleep Clinic, Human Sleep Project, BioSerenity network, and Central Disorders of Hypersomnolence registry: roughly 124,000 polysomnography recordings and more than 50 terabytes of multichannel physiology.

The design makes provenance a first-class output. Cohort rules and canonical data splits are encoded in the substrate, the agents reach it through a schema-validated Model Context Protocol layer, and every reported number, table, and figure is bound to an executable script. Human researchers still choose which hypotheses deserve a full run and review the resulting science.

The agents produced five lines of sleep science

The system's five case studies covered disease risk, physiological aging, comorbid insomnia and sleep apnea, REM regulation, and narcolepsy microstructure. In the Stanford discovery cohort, each one-standard-deviation decrease in N2 brain-to-brain coupling was associated with a 48% higher subsequent hazard of Parkinson's disease and a 38% higher hazard of Alzheimer's disease.

A physiologically structured late-fusion sleep-age model also reduced mean absolute error from 7.33 to 7.06 years at Stanford and from 9.52 to 8.83 years in external Human Sleep Project data without retraining. Across 2,496 disorder-feature pairs, the researchers reported cross-site effect-size concordance of r=0.631; the control feature landscapes were even more consistent at r=0.979.

This remains a retrospective, observational preprint. The disease findings generate hypotheses rather than causal claims, the cohorts combine referral and epidemiological populations, and none of the derived measures is yet a clinical decision tool. The next unlock is independent replication, prospective evaluation, and evidence that the findings remain stable across model versions and repeat agent runs.

RAG turned clinical notes into an extensible safety layer

A peer-reviewed JMIR study published August 10 applied retrieval-augmented generation to adverse-drug-event identification in deidentified Chinese clinical notes. The work began with 19,983 notes from multiple tertiary hospitals, produced a 2,510-record reference set and a 5,144-record ADE knowledge base, and released the LLMADE corpus and code.

The retrieval layer is the important advance. It can absorb new safety examples without retraining the core model, while structured labels and retrieved cases make classifications easier to inspect. In the low-prevalence evaluation, RAG reached 94.48% recall and 98.21% specificity; on the curated reference set, its level-3 F1 reached 0.9638.

External validation will determine how well the system travels. The knowledge base and evaluation notes came from the same overall source distribution, conventional clinical NLP was not tested head-to-head, and a 1.79% false-positive rate translates to roughly 17 false flags per 1,000 notes at the observed prevalence. Multicenter testing and measurement of reviewer workload are the practical next steps.

Open-weight agents brought cohort preparation onto local hardware

A second July 24 preprint, Agentic coding without the cloud, addresses a common constraint in longitudinal research: safeguarded data that cannot be sent to third-party model providers. RRBench evaluates locally deployed agents as they write and execute R code for 20 tasks creating 102 variables from six sweeps of a British cohort study.

Models in the 31-to-35-billion-parameter range completed up to 87.9% of the benchmark on consumer-grade hardware. The open RRBench repository includes task definitions, reference scripts, agent code, tests, and the table-comparison evaluator; the safeguarded cohort data remain separate.

The benchmark covers one cohort and measures preparation rather than downstream estimates, so generalization across datasets and analytical decisions is still to be established. Its contribution is a measurable local path: bring the agent to the data, execute inside an isolated environment, and compare outputs with an executable reference.

The next unlock is agentic infrastructure

Today's new signal is architectural. Read-only data access, local execution, curated retrieval, schema validation, executable provenance, and explicit expert checkpoints are turning agents into components of the evidence stack. These controls do not merely contain risk; they make ambitious scientific automation possible in settings where data scale and governance have historically limited what research teams could attempt.

The opportunity now is to test that infrastructure across institutions, model versions, and downstream decisions. When faster discovery travels with reproducible code, independent validation, and visible human authority, agentic AI can increase both the reach and the inspectability of real-world evidence.
