# Research notes - 2026-08-14

## Editorial line

**Headline:** The agents screened 5,598 papers. Their top first pass missed 43% of eligible studies.

MacAma is a peer-reviewed, open-source multi-agent workflow for systematic review and meta-analysis. Its radiotherapy case study spans retrieval, screening, effect-size analysis, plotting, and manuscript drafting. The authors estimate that the end-to-end workflow compressed more than 18 months of conventional work into under four days, but the strongest initial-screening agent by F1 recalled only 56.55% of eligible records. That tradeoff makes human verification part of the evidence system, not a temporary concession.

## Ranked evidence

### 1. MacAma: Multi-AI Agent as a Co-Scientist for Automated Meta-Analysis

- Direct sources: [peer-reviewed article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13431750/), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/smmd.70045), [open repository](https://github.com/YilinYuan/MacAma)
- Publication date: July 29, 2026
- Stable ID: `doi:10.1002/smmd.70045`
- PMID: `42549328`
- PMCID: `PMC13431750`
- Content version: `version-of-record`
- Internal score: 9/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. A multi-agent system performs a named evidence-lifecycle task—systematic review and meta-analysis—from literature retrieval through screening, quantitative synthesis, and report generation, with protocol-constrained human verification.
- What the primary sources establish:
  - MacAma organizes five workflows: literature retrieval, title/abstract screening, full-text screening, data analysis, and report writing.
  - The system encodes selected elements of PRISMA 2020, PICOS, and the SYRCLE risk-of-bias framework, records structured rationales, and keeps high-impact statistical and interpretive work subject to expert verification.
  - The case study retrieved 5,598 records about radiotherapy and anti-tumor immunity. The authors report that the workflow reduced manual screening burden by more than 80%.
  - On a held-out 354-record title/abstract benchmark, the DeepSeek-R1 workflow had the highest F1 (0.7069), with 94.25% precision and 56.55% recall. Qwen-max reached F1 0.7049, 86.87% precision, and 59.31% recall. SciBERT reached F1 0.6988, 72.24% precision, and 71.00% recall.
  - On a separate 599-record full-text benchmark limited to records with complete full text and human labels, MacAma reported 98.83% accuracy, 100% precision, 97.49% recall, and F1 0.9873. Ablations using zero-shot extraction or condensed screening instructions were materially worse.
  - The paper estimates initial screening at about 56 hours versus 9-12 months manually, full-text screening at about 24 hours versus 6-8 months, and the total workflow at under four days versus more than 1.5 years. These are case-study estimates supplied by the authors, not results from a randomized time-and-motion comparison.
  - The repository publishes workflow resources, screening code, benchmark materials, the radiotherapy case package, a statistical-analysis app, and an AI-assisted writing module under an MIT license. Running the workflow requires external model APIs and configured application IDs.
- Why it matters: the paper advances agentic evidence synthesis from a generic writing assistant toward an auditable, protocol-constrained research pipeline. It also shows why efficiency cannot be separated from error costs. A first-pass screen with 56.55% recall would miss 43.45% of eligible positive records in that benchmark unless a later control catches them.
- Limits:
  - The case study is preclinical radiotherapy evidence, not a clinical comparative-effectiveness review using patient-level RWD.
  - Initial-screening and full-text benchmarks are different tasks on different record sets and should not be compared as if performance improved on one continuous test set.
  - Only 599 of 749 obtainable full-text records were included in the full-text benchmark because complete text and human labels were required.
  - Chart extraction, effect-size choices, heterogeneity interpretation, and conclusions remain human-accountable steps.
  - Full-text access, retrieval quality, paywalls, domain transportability, and external validation remain limitations.
- Follow-up: independent replication in clinical and observational RWE reviews; prospective measurement of reviewer time and missed-study risk; comparison against dual-human screening; versioned protocol and model provenance; full reporting of disagreement and adjudication; validation of effect estimates and heterogeneity judgments, not just screening labels.

## Friday synthesis

Across the August 8-14 editions, the repeated signal was a widening gap between workflow scale and evidentiary control:

1. **Retrieval and screening are becoming agentic, but recall remains the first control surface.** The August 12 review workflow retrieved only 51% of the final evidence set; MacAma's highest-F1 first pass recalled 56.55% of eligible records.
2. **Accuracy becomes less persuasive as tasks become more interpretive.** Cohort extraction, cancer staging, safety-signal assessment, and meta-analysis each produced strong numbers on bounded subtasks while retaining uncertainty around completeness, transportability, or downstream inference.
3. **Human checkpoints are moving, not disappearing.** Agents can build cohorts, screen literature, draft analyses, and write reports. Humans remain responsible for source coverage, chart extraction, effect-size selection, heterogeneity, adjudication, and the final claim.
4. **Prospective evidence infrastructure is emerging.** FDA's TEMPO pilot places AI deployment and RWD collection in one regulatory-payment pathway, while research systems such as MacAma place provenance and review gates inside the analytic workflow.

The contradiction is now explicit: the largest reported efficiency gains arrive exactly where missing evidence can be hardest to notice. The frontier is not autonomous evidence production; it is verifiably bounded automation with visible error costs.
