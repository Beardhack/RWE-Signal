The Same Patient Data Produced Different Disability Scores

Two multiple-sclerosis studies show that an AI-derived RWE phenotype can drift in the model and in the rules that follow it

August 6, 2026 — Daily edition

A model can extract the same clinical facts twice and still leave researchers with different endpoints. The disagreement may enter after the extraction is finished.

A new multiple-sclerosis preprint held eight LLM-derived functional-system scores fixed across 57,604 clinical notes. It then calculated the same disability endpoint three ways. Two executable implementations agreed almost perfectly; a natural-language version run by an LLM diverged on roughly one in six notes.

A second study put GPT-4 inside a clinical decision tree to flag multiple-sclerosis diagnoses from first neurology notes. Structure improved inspectability, but hallucinations still appeared in 26% of the 125 cases. Together, the studies locate two separate validation targets: the model that reads the record, and the specification that turns its output into an RWE variable.

The endpoint changed after the inputs stopped changing

Sy Hwang and colleagues studied the Expanded Disability Status Scale, or EDSS, a standard multiple-sclerosis measure used in severity stratification, progression analysis, cohort definitions, and treatment research. Their design isolates a problem that is usually entangled with model accuracy: all three calculators received the same eight LLM-predicted subscores for every note.

One calculator asked an LLM to execute a neurologist-authored rubric written in natural language. A second used deterministic Python code produced with an LLM-assisted coding workflow and then frozen. The third was a port of an existing open-source EDSS implementation.

The deterministic pair matched exactly on 98.1% of notes and came within half a point on 99.8%. The natural-language calculator matched exactly on 84.2% against the executable version and 82.8% against the canonical version. The drift was concentrated in the mild-to-moderate range, where overlapping branches in the prose rubric left room for different interpretations.

Repeated calls did little to rescue the disagreement. Across five runs on every note, 9,106 notes had a median natural-language score different from the executable result. In 94.7% of those cases, the executable score remained outside the full range of all five responses. That is specification drift, not merely a bad roll of a stochastic model.

Prompt prose is part of the study instrument

The paper's strongest practical move was to search for concrete inputs that made the two deterministic implementations disagree. Exhaustive testing found seven minimal divergence patterns involving high non-ambulatory scores, tie-breaking, and the precedence of walking ability versus other functional systems. Five of those patterns appeared in the real cohort.

That makes endpoint implementation auditable in the same way as analysis code: version the rules, freeze the executable form, generate boundary cases, and measure whether revisions move patients across study-relevant categories. Simply stating that a prompt follows the EDSS rubric does not establish that two runs—or two representations—implement the same endpoint.

The evidence is still bounded. The functional-system inputs were generated by an LLM rather than taken as clinician ground truth, the canonical comparator embodies its own interpretation choices, and the preprint comes from one registry. The study isolates implementation drift; it does not prove that any one calculator reconstructs the true clinical state for every note.

A decision tree constrained GPT-4, but did not silence it

Shruthi Venkatesh and colleagues tested another way to narrow model discretion. They translated the 2017 McDonald diagnostic criteria into a decision tree, supplied expert-curated clinical knowledge, and asked GPT-4 to make a binary judgment at each node.

The input was the first neurology note from 125 clinic patients enrolled from 2017 through 2023. The researchers removed the assessment and plan, leaving clinical history and diagnostic testing. The final classifications were compared with neurologist-assessed diagnoses.

The structured system reached 84% accuracy, 79% precision, 74% recall, and 91% specificity. Yet reviewers found hallucinations in 32 cases—26% of the sample—with incoherence and overreliance the most common categories. A decision tree can reveal where reasoning failed; it cannot guarantee that the answer at each node is factual.

This is a proof of concept, not a portable phenotype. The enriched 125-person clinic sample does not reflect the prevalence of a general population, there was no external-site validation, and the study used the 2017 criteria rather than the revised 2024 framework. Before the flag becomes an RWE cohort variable, its error distribution needs to be tested where cohort inclusion actually turns.

What changed: validation split into two layers

The model and the endpoint specification are separate sources of uncertainty. Validating note extraction does not validate the calculator downstream; validating deterministic code does not validate the clinical judgments supplied to it.

The practical pattern is now clearer: preserve the evidence behind model decisions, constrain the task with explicit clinical logic, freeze executable endpoint rules, test boundary cases, and measure cohort reclassification after every material change. An AI-derived phenotype is not one model output. It is a chain of versioned decisions.
