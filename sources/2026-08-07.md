## Ranked evidence

### 1. Weisberg et al. - GPT-4o extraction of rheumatoid-arthritis disease activity

- Direct source: [JAMIA Open](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamiaopen/ooag152)
- Electronic publication: July 30, 2026
- Stable IDs: `doi:10.1093/jamiaopen/ooag152`; `pmid:42539747`
- Internal score: 9/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. The study evaluates zero-shot GPT-4o extraction of the Clinical Disease Activity Index and its components from real EHR notes for longitudinal rheumatoid-arthritis research.
- Watchlist connection: Chunhua Weng is a co-author.
- What the source establishes:
  - The study began with an institutional clinical data warehouse containing more than 4.5 million patients and identified 2,756 patients with rheumatoid arthritis and clinical notes.
  - The cohort included 1,038 seropositive patients, 796 seronegative patients, and 922 whose serostatus was unknown.
  - CDAI or its components were extractable for 160 of 1,038 seropositive patients, or 15.4%.
  - Against the study's validation set, the zero-shot GPT-4o workflow reported precision, recall, and F1 of 0.97.
  - The model ran through an institution-approved, HIPAA-compliant API environment.
- Why it matters: extraction quality and evidence availability are different validation dimensions. A model can reproduce documented scores accurately without making the underlying cohort longitudinally observable.
- Limits: single academic EHR; disease-activity documentation was sparse; the abstract does not establish portability across note styles, sites, or newer models; high extraction performance does not validate downstream comparative-effectiveness estimates.
- Follow-up: report both accuracy conditional on documentation and the fraction of the target cohort for which the intended variable can actually be derived.

### 2. Corso et al. - clinician-shaped prompts for local small models in lung-cancer EHRs

- Direct source: [Communications Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43856-026-01790-5)
- Publication date: July 16, 2026
- Stable IDs: `doi:10.1038/s43856-026-01790-5`; `pmid:42463788`
- Internal score: 9/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. The study evaluates locally deployable small language models for extracting treatment-relevant variables from the APOLLO real-world non-small-cell lung-cancer dataset.
- What the source establishes:
  - The real-world dataset contained 897 patients: 711 in an immunotherapy development cohort and 186 in a tyrosine-kinase-inhibitor validation cohort.
  - The study compared LLaMA 3.1 8B, Mistral 7B, BioMistral 7B, and MedLLaMA 2 7B across zero-shot, few-shot, and clinically annotated prompting.
  - Reported average F1 values across the evaluated extraction tasks were 0.62 for LLaMA, 0.54 for Mistral, 0.29 for BioMistral, and 0.24 for MedLLaMA.
  - Clinically annotated prompting pushed ECOG performance to 0.97 accuracy, while T-stage extraction remained weak: LLaMA ranged from 0.39 to 0.48 accuracy across prompting strategies.
  - Native-language prompting mattered. For smoking status, LLaMA accuracy improved from 48% with English to 72% with Italian in zero-shot prompting, and from 59% to 82% with few-shot prompting.
  - Twenty-six clinicians participated: 10 experienced oncologists, 8 residents, and 8 medical students. Prompt strategy changed agreement patterns across experience groups.
- Why it matters: model choice alone did not determine extraction quality. Clinical annotation, language, expertise, and the semantic form of the target variable all changed performance.
- Limits: one disease and dataset; a small clinician group; implicit TNM descriptions remained difficult; hallucinations occurred; external validation was limited to two models and one cohort.
- Follow-up: validate each field separately, preserve the prompt and annotation provenance, and avoid importing aggregate model rankings into a different variable or site.

### 3. Fong et al. - LLM-augmented anti-amyloid pharmacovigilance

- Direct source: [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42490993/)
- Publication date: July 16, 2026
- Stable IDs: `doi:10.1002/dad2.70412`; `pmid:42490993`
- Internal score: 8/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. The study evaluates an LLM-augmented workflow for post-market adverse-event surveillance across real clinical documents from patients receiving anti-amyloid therapy.
- What the source establishes:
  - The retrospective study reviewed 20,123 clinical documents from 46 patients who received at least one anti-amyloid dose in a large mid-Atlantic health system between June 24, 2024, and July 30, 2025.
  - Adjudication found an adverse event in 35 of 46 patients.
  - The LLM-augmented workflow reported 100% sensitivity and 89.7% positive predictive value.
  - Standard expert review reported 88.6% sensitivity and 100% positive predictive value.
- Why it matters: the model changed the operating point rather than simply outperforming human review. For safety surveillance, higher sensitivity may be useful if false-positive review burden remains manageable.
- Limits: preliminary, single-system study with only 46 patients; the abstract does not establish generalizability to other therapies, documentation systems, or prevalence settings; positive predictive value will change with event prevalence.
- Follow-up: measure reviewer workload, time saved, false-positive causes, site portability, and performance after deployment drift.

## Friday synthesis

Across this week's seven included studies, AI-plus-RWE reliability repeatedly met three ceilings:

1. **Observability:** the medication paper and today's rheumatoid-arthritis study show that a model cannot reconstruct evidence that was never captured or was lost in mapping.
2. **Specification:** EDSS implementation drift and weak lung-cancer T-stage extraction show that ambiguous clinical rules and implicit language can move derived variables even after data are available.
3. **Oversight and reproducibility:** EmulatRx, THESEUS, SHARE, and today's pharmacovigilance study point toward versioned workflows, human adjudication, and task-specific validation rather than a single general model score.

The evidence supports an inference, not a universal law: the most credible systems expose where evidence is missing, preserve how a variable was derived, and keep a review path for errors whose costs are asymmetric.
