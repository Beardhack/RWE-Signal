## Ranked evidence

### 1. Hwang et al. — specification drift in an LLM-derived endpoint

- Direct source: [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.06.25.26356350v1.full)
- Date: July 7, 2026
- Stable ID: `doi:10.64898/2026.06.25.26356350`
- Internal score: 9/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. The primary source evaluates an LLM-supported pipeline that derives a multiple-sclerosis disability endpoint from 57,604 real clinical notes for uses including cohort definition, severity stratification, progression analysis, and outcomes research.
- What the source establishes:
  - The cohort contained 57,604 progress notes from 5,231 patients in the Penn Neuroimmunology Registry.
  - A fixed LLM produced eight functional-system subscores per note. Those identical inputs were passed to a prompt-executed natural-language calculator, an LLM-generated deterministic Python calculator, and a canonical open-source implementation of the EDSS rubric.
  - The two deterministic calculators matched exactly on 98.1% of notes and were within 0.5 EDSS points on 99.8%.
  - The natural-language calculator matched exactly on 84.2% versus the executable implementation and 82.8% versus the canonical implementation.
  - In five-repeat testing across all 57,604 notes, 9,106 notes had a median natural-language score that differed from the executable score; 94.7% of those disagreements persisted across all five repeats.
  - Clinically pivotal threshold reclassification was rare at selected higher boundaries, but representation-driven drift concentrated in the mild-to-moderate EDSS range and can change cohort or severity assignments.
- Why it matters: endpoint logic is part of the study instrument. A derived RWE variable can drift even when the upstream extracted facts do not change, and repeated model calls do not repair most systematic disagreements.
- Limits: preprint; one disease registry; upstream functional-system scores were model-predicted rather than clinician ground truth; the canonical implementation also embodies interpretation choices; clinical notes were not public; downstream treatment-effect estimates were not compared.
- Follow-up: freeze and version executable endpoint specifications, test minimal counterexamples, measure boundary flips, and treat prompt-to-code changes as study changes.

### 2. Venkatesh et al. — GPT-4 inside a clinical decision tree

- Direct source: [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.14.26357416v1.full)
- Date: July 17, 2026
- Stable ID: `doi:10.64898/2026.07.14.26357416`
- Internal score: 8/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. The primary source uses GPT-4 for node-level reasoning in a computable phenotype framework applied to first neurology notes from a real clinic cohort; diagnosis labels of this kind are inputs to cohort construction and disease characterization.
- What the source establishes:
  - The study sampled first neurology notes from 125 patients enrolled between 2017 and 2023: 105 with multiple sclerosis, 10 with related disorders, and 10 controls.
  - Assessment and plan sections were redacted. The history and diagnostic-testing text was evaluated through a decision tree based on the 2017 McDonald criteria and expert-curated clinical knowledge.
  - GPT-4 made binary decisions at each node and achieved 84% accuracy, 79% precision, 74% recall, and 91% specificity against neurologist-assessed diagnoses.
  - Hallucinations appeared in 32 cases (26%); the most common categories were incoherence and overreliance.
- Why it matters: explicit clinical structure makes model reasoning inspectable, but it does not eliminate factual or logical failure. A phenotype flag needs error review and external validation before it becomes an RWE cohort variable.
- Limits: preprint; 125-person, highly enriched clinic sample; no external site or prospective validation; the study tested diagnostic flagging rather than a downstream comparative-effectiveness analysis; use of the 2017 rather than revised 2024 criteria limits current clinical interpretation.
- Follow-up: evaluate calibration and error rates in representative prevalence settings, test portability to new sites and notes, and quantify how misclassification changes cohort composition.

## What changed

The new signal is that constraining an LLM is necessary but not sufficient. The endpoint's representation can create systematic drift after extraction, while an explicit diagnostic tree can still carry hallucinations into cohort labels. Both layers need separate validation.
