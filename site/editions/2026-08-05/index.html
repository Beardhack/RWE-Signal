The Missing Medication Was Often a Mapping Problem

An LLM-assisted EHR study shows why exact mismatch is not the same as missing evidence; a second agent workflow turns phenotype disagreements into targeted expert review

August 5, 2026 — Daily edition

A medication listed in a clinic note but absent from the same visit's structured record looks like a data gap. New evidence suggests that interpretation is often too simple.

An August 3 preprint used an LLM-assisted reference set, human audits, deterministic normalization, and successively broader comparisons across 27,752 note-derived medication rows. Same-visit exact overlap was only 16.44%. Once terminology and time were considered, 90.34% connected to structured medication history within 30 days.

A separate human–AI phenotyping study reaches the same operational conclusion from another direction: labels used in real-world evidence are not clerical byproducts. They are evidence artifacts that need supporting text, explicit ambiguity, and a path back to expert judgment.

Exact mismatch was the beginning of the analysis

Mingyang Jiang and colleagues' August 3 preprint compared treatment-context medication evidence in outpatient notes with structured EHR medication history. Its central result is a warning against turning a failed exact match into a claim that the structured record missed the medication.

Only 16.44% of note-derived rows had an exact same-visit match. Cumulative overlap rose to 55.17% when same-visit semantic relationships were included, 90.34% when a 30-day window was added, and 96.03% across any structured-history window.

The residual mismatch was heterogeneous too. In a manual review of 100 examples from a stricter OMOP-backed no-overlap group, 80 were attributed to note-side mapping failure, six to structured-side undercapture, seven to extraction or reference noise, and seven to candidate note-only evidence. The honest endpoint is therefore a set of possible explanations, not a single missingness label.

The LLM proposed labels; deterministic rules carried the evaluation

The study used a locally deployed Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct model with deterministic decoding and schema-constrained output to bootstrap a note-grounded reference set. It did not treat those outputs as verified truth. Humans audited 3,188 of 27,752 reference rows, with difficult cases deliberately overrepresented, and separately sampled 1,000 previously unaudited rows to estimate residual reliability.

On 5,403 held-out mention rows, exact canonical agreement improved from 0.7226 with surface matching to 0.8429 after lexical cleanup and curated alias mapping. In the random audit, canonical-label agreement was 0.9210 among evaluable valid medication mentions, but treatment-action attribution was only 0.5326. Knowing which drug was mentioned proved much easier than knowing what happened to it.

The paper remains a preprint, and its private single-center environment, project-specific label space, incomplete manual annotation, and institution-shaped alias resource limit generalizability. The result is a calibrated workflow for evidence characterization, not a transportable ground-truth medication extractor.

SHARE makes disagreement part of phenotype production

The SHARE preprint, posted July 16, applies a human–agent co-learning loop to rheumatoid-arthritis disease activity. That phenotype is important for treatment-effect studies but is scattered through narrative notes and difficult to abstract consistently.

Experts first wrote a common review guideline. The agent filtered for informative notes, extracted cited evidence, assigned one of four disease-activity categories, and returned a rationale, confidence, and ambiguity flag. Human reviewers then adjudicated disagreements with the underlying evidence in view.

Adjudication changed 127 of 626 initial human labels and added 60 missed notes, producing a 686-note reference set. The final agent's mean absolute error improved from 0.406 to 0.291, and its ambiguity flag agreed with expert ambiguity designations with 92.1% accuracy. A tiered configuration using a lower-cost model for extraction and a stronger model for final reasoning matched the team's GPT-5.4 high-reasoning configuration while cutting estimated cost by 69% and compute time by 70%.

This is an internally evaluated preprint from one health-system setting, not proof of portability across diseases or institutions. Its strongest contribution is procedural: the model preserves evidence and uncertainty, while experts own the guideline, adjudication, local validation, and final interpretation.

What changed: labels became reviewable evidence

The two studies move beyond asking whether an LLM can extract a clinical fact. They ask whether the path from text to an RWE variable can survive inspection.

The recurring design is now sharper: use models to find and structure evidence, require outputs to cite their support, expose uncertainty, test deterministic transformations on held-out patients, and send unstable cases back to people. The new signal is that disagreement itself can improve the evidence artifact—if it is preserved rather than averaged away.
