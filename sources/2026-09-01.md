## Ranked evidence

### 1. An LLM enabled real-time estimation of seasonal influenza vaccine effectiveness from social media data

- Direct source: [medRxiv v1](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.28.26361670v1) and [public analysis repository](https://github.com/pavia27/flu-ve-social-media-llm).
- First public date: August 31, 2026.
- Authors: Michael J. Pavia, Ivan Flores Amaro, Dongfang Xu, Graciela Gonzalez-Hernandez, and Matthew Scotch; Arizona State University and Cedars-Sinai.
- Stable ID: `doi:10.64898/2026.08.28.26361670`; story ID: `llm-social-media-influenza-ve-surveillance-2026`; content version: `v1`.
- Internal score: 9/10.
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes because LLaMA-3-70B-Instruct classifies vaccination and influenza-test status from public patient posts, after which a test-negative-like observational design estimates real-world seasonal vaccine effectiveness.**
- What the source establishes:
  - The pipeline uses few-shot chain-of-thought prompting with LLaMA-3-70B-Instruct to label self-reported vaccination status and influenza test results from public Twitter/X posts.
  - For 2024-25, 42,994 posts from 34,070 unique users yielded 597 eligible users: 547 test-positive, 50 test-negative, and 73 vaccinated. Crude VE was 29% (95% CI -69% to 67%; P=.4).
  - For 2025-26, 199,054 flu-test-collection posts from 136,058 unique users yielded 1,911 eligible users: 1,813 positive, 98 negative, and 800 vaccinated. Crude VE was 43% (95% CI 14% to 62%; P=.01).
  - Restricting the second season to the same period as CDC interim surveillance produced 41% VE (95% CI 3% to 64%; P=.03), within the CDC interim range.
  - The cumulative estimate first entered and remained within the CDC interim range on January 10, 2026, roughly nine weeks before the interim CDC report; statistical significance arrived five weeks later.
  - The MIT-licensed repository supplies derivative labels, de-identified user-level data, CDC surveillance data, R analysis code, a robustness probe, and figure-generation materials.
- Limits: this is a non-peer-reviewed preprint. Estimates are unadjusted for age, comorbidities, and care-seeking; the test-negative groups are small and imbalanced. Current-season classifier labels were not independently revalidated in this paper. The public repository starts from classifier output and omits the upstream collection and prompting pipeline, does not pin R dependencies, and does not include the code that produced one season's user-level table.

### 2. Emergency-department phenotyping from clinical text

- Direct source: [medRxiv v1](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.26.26361443v1).
- First public date: August 31, 2026.
- Authors: C. Humphries and colleagues.
- Stable ID: `doi:10.64898/2026.08.26.26361443`; story ID: `llm-ed-clinical-text-phenotyping-2026`; content version: `v1`.
- Internal score: 9/10.
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes because a locally deployed LLM turns unstructured emergency-department notes into structured alcohol-, drug-, and self-harm phenotypes, validates those labels against adjudicated records, and applies the workflow to a full year of routine-care encounters.**
- What the source establishes:
  - In a 2,256-attendance validation week, the adjudicated reference standard found 12.1% of attendances involved alcohol, drugs, or self-harm, versus 6.0% from coding, 10.0% from clinician review, and 15.6% from the LLM.
  - Balanced accuracy for LLM versus clinician review was 0.942 versus 0.930 for alcohol (P=.635), 0.959 versus 0.791 for drugs (P<.001), and 0.982 versus 0.908 for self-harm (P=.004).
  - Applied to 105,096 annual attendances at the same UK emergency department, the system estimated 12,890 domain involvements per year were not identifiable from coded data.
  - The model was locally deployed within NHS infrastructure, allowing text-derived phenotyping at a scale the authors describe as infeasible for manual review.
- Limits: this is a single-site, non-peer-reviewed diagnostic-accuracy study. The accessible abstract does not establish transportability to other documentation styles or institutions, and the LLM estimate exceeded the adjudicated validation-week prevalence.

### 3. LLM-assisted evidence audit of late-stage cancer incidence as a screening-trial endpoint

- Direct source: [medRxiv v1](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.29.26361733v1).
- First public date: August 31, 2026.
- Authors: S. Li and colleagues.
- Stable ID: `doi:10.64898/2026.08.29.26361733`; story ID: `llm-cancer-screening-endpoint-evidence-audit-2026`; content version: `v1`.
- Internal score: 9/10.
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes because Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview prioritizes a reconstructed trial-literature corpus for blinded human reassessment, directly changing the evidence base used to evaluate a candidate cancer-screening endpoint.**
- What the source establishes:
  - The researchers reconstructed the PubMed corpus behind a 41-comparison review, screened 1,209 records, and assessed 996 PDFs.
  - The LLM was used only to prioritize reports for blinded human reassessment. Humans determined eligibility, linked reports from the same trial, harmonized endpoints, and verified comparison-level data.
  - Of 33 absent reports prioritized by the model, 26 were eligible, representing 18 trials; eight supplied compatible comparisons.
  - Adding those comparisons expanded the analysis from 41 to 49 and reduced the overall trial-level correlation between late-stage incidence and cancer-specific mortality from 0.73 (95% CI 0.55-0.85) to 0.59 (95% CI 0.37-0.75).
  - Updated cancer-specific correlations were 0.49 for breast cancer, -0.23 for colorectal cancer, and 0.83 for lung cancer, with wide intervals and one sparse-event colorectal comparison exerting influence.
- Limits: this is a non-peer-reviewed evidence audit, not a validation of late-stage incidence as a universal surrogate. The LLM prioritized records but did not make final eligibility, reconciliation, endpoint, or extraction decisions.

### 4. Comparative performance of LLMs for appraising bias in RWE studies

- Direct source: [PubMed 42640121](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42640121/) and DOI `10.18553/jmcp.2026.32.9.1062`.
- Publication: September 2026 issue; the record was indexed August 25, 2026.
- Authors: Chijioke M. Okeke, Regina Nechi, Javeria Khalid, Ibraheem M. Karaye, J. Douglas Thornton, and Ismaeel Yunusa.
- Stable ID: `doi:10.18553/jmcp.2026.32.9.1062`; PMID: `42640121`; story ID: `llm-rwe-bias-appraisal-benchmark-2026`; content version: `version-of-record`.
- Internal score: 9/10.
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes because 40 LLMs perform item-level methodological bias appraisal on published pharmacoepidemiologic RWE studies against a human-adjudicated APPRAISE reference standard.**
- What the source establishes:
  - Forty models from six providers appraised ten RWE studies across 28 APPRAISE items, producing 280 item-level assessments per model.
  - Overall accuracy ranged from 12.9% to 66.1%; Claude Sonnet 4.6 led accuracy at 66.1%, followed by o3 at 65.4% and Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview at 65.0%.
  - Macro-F1 ranged from 30.9% to 66.9%; o3 led at 66.9%.
  - Two independent pharmacoepidemiology reviewers and a third adjudicator supplied the reference standard.
  - Human reviewers averaged 61.05 minutes per study, versus 0.80-17.22 minutes for the models.
- Limits: the benchmark contains ten studies, uses one structured appraisal framework and one prompting design, and does not report repeated-run reproducibility. Performance varied materially by model.

### 5. GPAgentBench-2K

- Direct source: [arXiv v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30188).
- First public date: August 31, 2026.
- Authors: Boqi Chen, Xudong Liu, Yunke Ao, Heejin Do, and Jianing Qiu.
- Stable ID: `arxiv:2608.30188`; story ID: `gpagentbench-clinical-agent-rwd-2026`; content version: `v1`.
- Internal score: 8/10.
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes because the benchmark evaluates LLM agents on six-action primary-care workflows constructed from expert-validated records of real-world general-practice encounters, including safety-constrained abstention.**
- What the source establishes:
  - GPAgentBench-2K models clinical decision-making as a constrained Markov decision process rather than a static prediction task.
  - It uses expert-validated records of real-world general-practice encounters and evaluates a six-action clinical workflow.
  - Sixteen LLMs were tested. The abstract reports performance degradation as the action space grew and says that even the frontier models with the highest diagnostic accuracy violated safety constraints in more than half of high-risk cases.
  - A constrained group-relative policy-optimization baseline improved performance over unconstrained reinforcement-learning methods.
- Limits: this is a non-peer-reviewed benchmark, not a prospective clinical deployment. The accessible abstract does not establish effects on patient outcomes or cross-system transportability.
