FDA Put an AI Program on a Real-World Evidence Contract

Dexcom's first-in-pilot glucose program will enter Medicare-linked care while its maker collects outcome data—a consequential shift whose protocol is still largely undisclosed

August 13, 2026 — Daily edition

The most important part of the new AI program is not the algorithm. It is the bargain around it.

The Food and Drug Administration selected Dexcom as the first participant in a pilot that pairs access to technology-supported chronic care with the production of real-world evidence. The company's glucose program is intended to combine continuous-sensor data with information about nutrition, physical activity, sleep, and stress, then use AI to provide personalized insights to patients and clinicians.

At the same time, the manufacturer will collect, monitor, and report real-world data tied to the program's intended uses. The pilot therefore puts an AI-enabled health product, a Medicare payment model, and an evidence-generation plan inside the same operating system.

That is a meaningful change in how the evidence might be produced. It is not yet evidence that the product improves health.

The AI and the evidence plan now share a pathway

Dexcom's Glucose Health Program is intended to support two clinical uses inside the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services' ACCESS model. FDA says the program may aid screening for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes and may contribute to better glycemic control and lower HbA1c among people with prediabetes.

Dexcom says its system will integrate readings from G7 and Stelo glucose sensors with contextual health data and use its clinician software to support monitoring and personalized care at population scale. Those are intended capabilities described by the company, not results from the pilot.

ACCESS began in July as a ten-year CMS Innovation Center test of outcome-aligned payment for technology-supported chronic care. Participating organizations are paid for measurable improvements rather than a fixed list of activities. TEMPO supplies the regulatory side: manufacturers offer selected digital-health devices while generating the RWD FDA and CMS need to judge how they perform in ordinary settings.

The result is an evidence loop, not the usual sequence in which a product is studied, authorized, reimbursed, and only later examined in routine use. Payment and deployment create the data environment; the data are meant to inform continued regulatory and coverage decisions.

The program is concrete. The study design is not.

FDA's announcement describes what it will consider when selecting TEMPO participants: potential risk, reasonable expectation of benefit, plans for RWD collection and analysis, and alignment with outcomes in ACCESS. The agency intends to select up to about ten participants in each of four clinical-use areas.

For the first participant, however, the public record stops before the details that would determine whether the evidence can support a causal or regulatory conclusion.

Screening for prediabetes is not the same endpoint as lowering HbA1c. Personalized insights are not the same intervention as the glucose sensor itself. An outcome-aligned payment can change engagement, adherence, and selection into the program. Without a prespecified design, an observed improvement could reflect the AI, the sensor, coaching, patient motivation, regression to the mean, or the care model surrounding all of them.

The broader TEMPO framework allows a participant to request enforcement discretion for some requirements while real-world performance data are collected. FDA has not publicly specified which requirements, if any, will apply to Dexcom's program. That makes transparency about monitoring, model changes, and decision thresholds especially important.

The real test is whether the learning is auditable

The significance of TEMPO is that it treats evidence generation as part of the product's route into care. If it works, regulators and payers could learn from actual use sooner, and patients could gain access without waiting for every question to be answered in a conventional premarket study.

The risk is that a learning system becomes a black box around another black box: an adaptive product evaluated through an undisclosed evidence plan. RWD volume will not solve that. The pilot needs a clear cohort, outcome definitions, a credible comparison, documentation of algorithm changes, and public reporting of failures as well as improvements.

FDA and CMS have created the setting for AI to earn evidence in the real world. The next signal will be whether they publish enough of the rules to let the evidence be judged.
