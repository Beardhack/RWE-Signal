# Research notes - 2026-08-11

## Editorial line

**Headline:** The LLM built a 17,223-patient cohort. The guideline's middle still collapsed.

Two August 10 preprints moved the AI-plus-RWE conversation from generic extraction claims to task-specific validation on routine clinical records. The stronger study used an independently evaluated LLM abstraction framework to assemble a three-system pulmonary-embolism cohort, then showed that a new consensus risk framework separated the extremes but not the intermediate strata where treatment choices are hardest. A second study found that a large open-source model could identify PCI reports accurately, yet its positive predictive value for complex PCI was only 57.6%.

## Ranked evidence

### 1. Alwakeel et al. - LLM-enabled cohort validation found a strong mortality staircase and a flat intermediate band

- Direct source: [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.06.26359865v1)
- Publication date: August 10, 2026
- Stable ID: `doi:10.64898/2026.08.06.26359865`
- Content version: `v1`
- Internal score: 9/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. A previously evaluated LLM abstraction framework converted routine radiology reports into imaging phenotypes that were joined with structured EHR data across three health systems to test a new pulmonary-embolism risk framework in 17,223 real patients.
- What the source establishes:
  - The pooled cohort contained 17,223 confirmed PE patients: Duke n=12,992, INSPECT/Stanford n=3,870, and MIMIC-IV/Beth Israel Deaconess n=361.
  - At Duke, the cohort was derived from 95,760 consecutive inpatient CTPAs from 2014 through 2025, preserving a full imaging denominator.
  - The LLM framework abstracted PE presence and location, right-heart strain, and study quality from radiology reports. Those outputs were combined with EHR biomarkers, imaging, procedures, and mortality.
  - Pooled 30-day mortality rose across the broad AHA/ACC categories: B 1.5%, C 8.9%, D 15.5%, and E 31.9%. The ordering held in all three cohorts.
  - The full subcategories did not improve discrimination over a collapsed intermediate band at Duke: C-index 0.696 versus 0.697.
  - Duke mortality in the middle was nonmonotonic: C1 9.2%, C2 10.8%, C3 8.1%, and D1 10.9%.
  - Missingness was clinically informative. Among Duke category-C patients, 967 of 7,633 lacked both echocardiography and biomarkers; their mortality was 10.4%. In INSPECT, 643 of 2,089 category-C patients were similarly indeterminate, with 7.3% mortality.
  - The authors report clear value at the extremes and advise caution when interpreting fine-grained intermediate subcategories.
- Supporting validation: the underlying [peer-reviewed LLM abstraction study](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-032-09569-5_21) evaluated 250 dual-annotated CTPA reports each from Duke and MIMIC-IV. Model mean accuracy ranged from 0.83 to 0.98, and dual-model agreement produced high predictive values for PE presence. Right-heart-strain agreement with human annotation was materially weaker, with kappa around 0.48-0.51.
- Why it matters: the paper demonstrates a credible downstream RWE use of LLM-derived phenotypes at multi-system scale, while showing that cohort scale cannot rescue a risk taxonomy whose clinically important middle categories do not separate.
- Limits: preprint; retrospective design; expert-consensus guideline categories; missing tests treated as criteria not met; LLM imaging errors can propagate into categories; right-heart-strain extraction was less reliable than PE-presence extraction; vasopressor and arrest criteria may reflect competing causes; treatment confounding was not adjusted; external cohorts had incomplete echocardiography and procedure data; MIMIC-IV was small.
- Follow-up: prospectively test the intermediate categories with explicit missingness states, adjudicate causes of shock and death, quantify phenotype-error propagation, and compare decisions made with the full versus collapsed framework.

### 2. Bhatt et al. - high report-level accuracy concealed weak precision for the complex phenotype

- Direct source: [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.08.05.26359802v1)
- Publication date: August 10, 2026
- Stable ID: `doi:10.64898/2026.08.05.26359802`
- Content version: `v1`
- Internal score: 9/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. The study evaluates open-source LLMs on de-identified cardiac-catheterization reports from routine care for scalable complex-PCI phenotyping and observational research.
- What the source establishes:
  - The test set contained 1,412 reports from three hospitals in Yale New Haven Health: 596 PCI and 816 non-PCI.
  - Four interventional-cardiology fellows annotated the reports, and a board-certified interventional cardiologist adjudicated the reference standard.
  - Llama 3.3 70B, Meditron-7B, and BioMistral-7B ran locally in a secure A100 environment with a standardized prompt and fixed JSON output.
  - For PCI identification, Llama achieved 100.0% sensitivity, 93.8% specificity, 92.1% PPV, 100.0% NPV, 96.4% accuracy, and 95.9% F1 across 1,386 evaluable reports.
  - Performance did not transfer from the large model to the two 7B medical models. Meditron-7B had 87.2% sensitivity but 7.8% specificity; BioMistral-7B had 1.2% sensitivity and 99.4% specificity.
  - For complex-PCI classification, Llama achieved 97.7% sensitivity, 80.1% specificity, 57.6% PPV, 99.2% NPV, 83.9% accuracy, and 72.5% F1 across 590 evaluable PCI reports.
  - Exact-match extraction accuracy for Llama ranged from 80.7% for lesion count to 96.8% for stent count.
  - Seventy percent of PCI-identification false positives were IVUS- or FFR-only procedures mistaken for intervention.
  - Site-level complex-PCI accuracy varied from 75.6% to 88.2%, indicating material documentation heterogeneity even within one health system.
- Why it matters: a 96.4% report-level accuracy headline does not describe the harder downstream phenotype. With PPV of 57.6%, many model-positive complex-PCI records would be false positives, which could distort comparative-effectiveness cohorts and risk estimates if accepted without review.
- Limits: preprint; retrospective; one health system despite three hospitals; no prospective workflow test; code and data unavailable because of privacy and IRB restrictions; minimal prompt development; only three models; rare-variable accuracy benefited from mostly negative labels; marked site heterogeneity.
- Follow-up: externally validate across health systems, report calibrated uncertainty, prospectively route only uncertain or positive complex cases to review, and measure how phenotype errors change study estimates.

## What changed

The field is producing more credible examples of LLM-derived variables being used in actual observational cohorts. The remaining bottleneck is no longer simply extraction accuracy: it is whether task-specific errors, missingness, and site heterogeneity alter the clinical categories or comparative-effectiveness conclusions built from those variables.
