An LLM Turned Public Flu Posts Into an Early Vaccine-Effectiveness Signal

Five new studies show language models moving into evidence production, phenotyping, audit, appraisal, and realistic clinical-agent evaluation—each with a human or statistical reference point.

RWE-Signal  ·  September 1, 2026  ·  Tuesday edition

Language models are becoming working components of the evidence stack. The strongest new examples do not ask an LLM for a final scientific answer. They give it a bounded job—classify patient reports, structure clinical text, prioritize a literature corpus, or apply an appraisal rubric—and connect that output to a statistical design or expert-adjudicated reference.

The clearest result arrived in an August 31 influenza vaccine-effectiveness preprint. An LLM labeled vaccination and flu-test evidence in public social-media posts, feeding a test-negative-like analysis whose 2025–26 estimate entered the CDC's interim range roughly nine weeks before the agency's report. Four companion studies extend the pattern across emergency-department phenotyping, cancer-screening evidence audit, RWE bias appraisal, and clinical-agent evaluation.

Public posts became an epidemiologic pipeline

Michael Pavia and colleagues used LLaMA-3-70B-Instruct with few-shot chain-of-thought prompting to identify self-reported influenza vaccination and test results in public Twitter/X posts. A user counted as vaccinated only when the evidence preceded a flu test by at least 14 days; logistic regression then converted vaccination odds among test-positive and test-negative users into a crude vaccine-effectiveness estimate.

The 2025–26 analysis began with 199,054 posts from 136,058 users and produced 1,911 eligible people: 1,813 test-positive, 98 test-negative, and 800 vaccinated. Its full-season estimate was 43% VE, with a 95% confidence interval of 14% to 62%. Restricting the data to the CDC interim surveillance period produced 41% VE, within the agency's interim range.

The cumulative estimate first entered and remained within that range on January 10, about nine weeks before the CDC interim report; statistical significance arrived five weeks later. That timing is the practical promise: passively observed public data may complement clinic-based surveillance with an earlier directional read, not replace it.

The evidence boundary is visible and useful. This is a non-peer-reviewed, unadjusted analysis with small control groups and no independent validation of the current-season labels. The MIT-licensed repository publishes derivative labels, user-level data, CDC inputs, and analysis code, but starts after the collection and LLM-labeling steps. Closing that reproducibility gap—and adjusting for age, comorbidity, care-seeking, and platform selection—is the next route to a stronger surveillance instrument.

Clinical text exposed the burden that codes could not see

A second August 31 medRxiv study tested whether a locally deployed LLM could structure alcohol-, drug-, and self-harm involvement from emergency-department notes. In a 2,256-attendance validation week, a conflict-adjudicated reference standard found at least one of those domains in 12.1% of encounters. Clinical coding found 6.0%; clinician review found 10.0%; the LLM identified 15.6%.

Balanced accuracy was 0.942 for the LLM versus 0.930 for clinician review on alcohol, 0.959 versus 0.791 on drugs, and 0.982 versus 0.908 on self-harm. The drug and self-harm differences were statistically significant. Applied to 105,096 attendances over a full year at the same UK emergency department, the model produced structured phenotypes at a scale the authors say is not feasible for manual review and estimated 12,890 annual domain involvements that codes could not identify.

This is a single-site preprint, so transportability across documentation styles remains open; the LLM also identified a higher share than the adjudicated validation-week reference. Still, the local NHS deployment demonstrates a concrete RWD capability: unstructured notes can become a measurable surveillance layer without sending text to an external model service.

An LLM-assisted audit changed the screening-endpoint evidence base

In cancer screening, the emerging question is whether late-stage incidence can serve as an earlier trial endpoint than cancer-specific mortality. An August 31 evidence-audit preprint reconstructed the PubMed corpus behind a 41-comparison review and used Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview only to prioritize reports for blinded human reassessment.

Of 33 reports absent from the source review and prioritized by the model, human reviewers found 26 eligible, representing 18 trials; eight provided compatible comparisons. Adding them expanded the analysis from 41 to 49 comparisons and attenuated the overall correlation from 0.73 to 0.59. Cancer-specific estimates diverged: 0.49 for breast, −0.23 for colorectal, and 0.83 for lung cancer, all with uncertainty that made disease-specific interpretation essential.

The workflow is capability-led and deliberately bounded. Humans made every eligibility decision, linked multiple reports from the same trial, harmonized endpoints, and verified the extracted values. The LLM made a large corpus more inspectable; source verification turned that prioritization into evidence.

Forty models mapped the speed and ceiling of RWE bias appraisal

A peer-reviewed Journal of Managed Care & Specialty Pharmacy study tested 40 LLMs from six providers on ten published RWE studies. Each model completed 28 items from the APPRAISE bias framework, producing 280 item-level assessments per model. Two pharmacoepidemiology reviewers and a third adjudicator supplied the human reference standard.

Overall accuracy ranged from 12.9% to 66.1%. Claude Sonnet 4.6 led accuracy at 66.1%, while o3 led macro-F1 at 66.9%. The efficiency spread was equally informative: humans averaged 61.05 minutes per study, while models took 0.80 to 17.22 minutes.

The immediate opportunity is a fast first appraisal or second-reader layer, with model choice visible and expert adjudication retained. The ten-study benchmark is small, performance varied substantially, and repeated-run reproducibility remains untested; a larger, multi-framework evaluation can now build on a concrete human-referenced baseline.

A primary-care benchmark gave agents a realistic action space

GPAgentBench-2K, posted August 31, moves clinical-agent evaluation beyond static question answering. It builds a safety-constrained, six-action primary-care workflow from expert-validated records of real-world general-practice encounters and treats abstention as an explicit outcome.

Across 16 LLMs, performance fell as the action space became more complex. A constrained group-relative policy-optimization baseline improved on unconstrained reinforcement learning, demonstrating that workflow and safety constraints can be trained into the agent loop. The benchmark also reports that even the frontier models with the strongest diagnostic accuracy violated safety constraints in more than half of high-risk cases.

That result identifies the next engineering target without diminishing the advance: realistic action spaces make it possible to optimize diagnosis, sequence, and abstention together. Because this is a non-peer-reviewed benchmark rather than a prospective deployment, patient-outcome validation is still ahead.

The new signal is a disciplined division of labor

Today's genuinely new signal is an architecture recurring across five independent studies. Language models find, classify, prioritize, or structure difficult evidence at scale. Statistical designs, adjudicated references, and domain experts determine whether that output becomes a defensible estimate, phenotype, corpus, appraisal, or clinical action.

Bound the taskGive the model a specific classification, retrieval, appraisal, or action role.
Keep a referenceAnchor performance to human adjudication, a statistical design, or explicit safety constraints.
Expose the handoffPublish provenance and artifacts so model output can be checked before it becomes evidence.

That division of labor is a credible route from promising automation to evidence infrastructure. The next unlock is not removing the human or statistical layer; it is making every model-to-evidence handoff faster, more reproducible, and easier to audit.
