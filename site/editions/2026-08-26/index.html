LLMs Turn Clinical Notes Into Future Queries and Structured Evidence

Three new benchmarks advance the evidence pipeline from longitudinal outcomes to coded facts and evidence-bounded terminology.

RWE-Signal  ·  August 26, 2026  ·  Daily edition

A new cluster of studies is making the unstructured clinical record more computable. One framework converts longitudinal ICU notes into time-indexed questions about what happened next; two others benchmark the extraction and terminology-linking layers that turn narrative documentation into reusable evidence inputs.

The shared advance is architectural. Instead of asking a model for an unconstrained clinical summary, these systems define an observable task, preserve a reference answer or terminology source, and measure the model against patient histories or human annotations. That creates clearer handoffs for RWE teams deciding where LLMs can already accelerate work and where verification should remain explicit.

All three artifacts became public on August 24. Two are medRxiv preprints, and the trajectory paper is an arXiv preprint accepted to a MICCAI workshop; none is a completed prospective deployment study.

A medical world model learned to query what happened next

Future Querying reframes clinical forecasting as a natural-language evidence task. The system reads a patient's observed notes, receives a time-indexed question, and predicts an answer that can be checked against the hidden future portion of the same longitudinal record.

The August 24 v1 paper constructs its real-world benchmark from de-identified MIMIC-IV discharge summaries and radiology reports. It includes 9,733 ICU patients and 164,911 questions, with patient-wise training, validation, and test splits. The task spans six semantic categories and does not require a separately engineered outcome model for every question.

Task-specific LoRA fine-tuning improved the three tested small open models by 11.7 to 21.5 percentage points on MIMIC and brought their accuracy into a 43% to 46% range. Among the off-the-shelf frontier systems, only Claude Sonnet 5 and GLM-5.2 narrowly exceeded 50% on the real-note benchmark.

The authors use Gemini 2.5 Flash as the answer judge and manually check a subset, while explicitly treating the score as a comparison between approaches rather than absolute clinical correctness. Clinical-expert validation, a human upper bound, uncertainty estimates, and tests that separate learned dynamics from pattern matching remain the next evidence layer. The practical opportunity is still substantial: a locally fine-tuned model can reuse one longitudinal text representation across many outcome questions.

A seven-model benchmark mapped ENT notes to SNOMED

A separate August 24 medRxiv preprint evaluates seven LLMs against 14 medical professionals on 98 public ENT clinical documents. Each reader extracted seven categories—including symptoms, diagnoses, treatments, risk factors, and test results—and assigned SNOMED-CT codes.

The design matters for RWE because it measures structured extraction against the variation between human annotators, rather than treating one annotation file as unquestionable ground truth. It also separates reliability from precision and recall, giving implementers more than one view of extraction quality.

Clinician-to-clinician kappa was 0.752, compared with 0.391 between LLMs and clinicians. A Bayesian analysis estimated clinician agreement at 0.813 and clinician-LLM agreement at 0.659; non-inferiority was not established at the prespecified margins. GPT-4o nevertheless reached 97.0% precision and 84.9% recall, a profile that supports the authors' proposed near-term role: high-throughput first-pass extraction with human verification.

The benchmark is narrow—98 public ENT documents, one terminology system, and annotators who are also study authors. Its value is a reusable evaluation structure. Larger multisite datasets can now test whether the same precision and category-level behavior carry into operational RWE pipelines.

Evidence-bounded linking separated retrieval from invention

The third August 24 pilot breaks clinical terminology work into distinct stages: find a candidate span, retrieve controlled terminology evidence, decide whether a link is supported, and triage genuinely unmatched terms for possible ontology extension.

Across 66 de-identified notes from three health systems, MedGemma produced the highest automated embedding-match F1 at 0.55; human inter-annotator embedding-match F1 was 0.75. Exact-string scores were much lower for both humans and models, reinforcing the paper's point that boundary-sensitive matching and semantic agreement answer different questions.

The retrieval experiment is the stronger systems result. With complete evidence, the pipeline returned the reference-matched concept for all 28 known-concept spans. When matched concepts were hidden, the LLM-only condition still declared three spans linked, while 25 were sent to a possible-candidate state. In a follow-up set of 84 previously unmatched terms, full vector retrieval classified every term as a plausible existing concept and proposed none for extension, although those extension decisions were not independently adjudicated.

That stage separation offers a practical governance pattern: retain the extracted span, the retrieved terminology evidence, the model's decision, and the escalation state as distinct artifacts. RWE teams can then improve or audit one layer without silently rewriting the others.

The new signal is a composable evidence layer

Today's releases do not establish autonomous clinical evidence generation. They do make the component architecture more concrete. One system creates answerable questions from longitudinal outcomes; another measures how reliably models can turn prose into coded facts; the third requires terminology evidence before a concept link becomes durable.

The opportunity is to combine those components without losing their checkpoints. Flexible LLMs can broaden the questions asked of real-world records, while explicit references, controlled vocabularies, patient-wise evaluation, and human review keep the outputs inspectable. The next unlock is multisite validation showing that these layers remain reliable when documentation style, patient mix, and evidence purpose change.
