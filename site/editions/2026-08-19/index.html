A Pan-Cancer Model Carried Real-World Learning Across Hospitals

PanoraOnc transfers clinico-genomic outcome modeling across institutions, while a local LLM pipeline makes AML adverse-event coding traceable from free text to MedDRA.

RWE-Signal  ·  August 19, 2026  ·  Daily edition

Two new studies show foundation models becoming reusable parts of the evidence stack. One learns across 84,131 real-world cancer patients and carries that learning into cohorts it has not seen. The other turns variable safety language from three controlled leukemia trials into a clinically reviewed terminology decision.

The lead development is PanoraOnc, a pan-cancer model posted August 18. Its strongest result is not a single outcome score. It is transfer: large-scale real-world pretraining improved zero-shot prediction across institutions, therapies, and cancer types, then reduced the amount of local data needed to reach useful performance.

PanoraOnc treated real-world cohorts as reusable pretraining

The model starts with three complementary patient sources. Dana-Farber contributed 58,694 patients across 66 cancers. Memorial Sloan Kettering contributed 24,950 patients across five cancers. A third melanoma cohort, 8RNAWES, contributed 487 patients with whole-exome and RNA measurements. Together, the study covers 84,131 people, with modalities that include clinical histories, treatments, targeted sequencing, whole-exome sequencing, RNA, and imaging-derived immune features.

Each case was represented as a structured, JSON-like patient record for a Gemma3-12B vision-language base model adapted with low-rank training. The authors anchored prediction at a treatment decision point and supplied only pretreatment features. The model then predicted treatment response or survival at one, two, and three years.

A direct model trained on one target cohort improved markedly over the unadapted base, but it needed enough local cases to learn the setting. Pan-cancer pretraining changed that starting point. On an external MSK pan-cancer and pan-treatment cohort, a model pretrained only on Dana-Farber data reached a zero-shot AUROC of 0.77—0.18 above the base model—without using MSK patients during pretraining.

The few-shot experiments make the transfer effect concrete. For two-year survival after immunotherapy in Dana-Farber non-small-cell lung cancer, combined Dana-Farber-and-MSK pretraining reached AUROC 0.69 before receiving target-cohort examples. It remained ahead of a model trained directly on that cohort until roughly 1,020 local patients were available. Crossover occurred with fewer local samples in several other cohorts.

More modalities could add information beyond the clinical record. In the largest multimodal comparison, the all-feature model reached AUROC 0.82 versus 0.77 for clinical variables alone. Integrated-gradient analyses surfaced both familiar and less-studied genomic and immune features, offering candidate directions for follow-up rather than declaring new biomarkers.

The study also turns the model into an observational research instrument. Across 6,282 Dana-Farber and MSK patients with non-small-cell lung cancer, PanoraOnc generated factual and hypothetical outcomes under chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy. Propensity modeling and targeted maximum likelihood estimation then supported subgroup exploration in an interactive research agent called Dr. PanoraOnc.

That extension is promising precisely because its scientific status is clear. The counterfactual results depend on treatment overlap and no unmeasured confounding; they are hypotheses, not treatment recommendations. Endpoint definitions differ across the real-world cohorts, the interactive agent has not yet been prospectively evaluated, and candidate biomarkers need independent validation. MSK-based weights are open, while consent and IRB terms prevent release of the Dana-Farber-trained weights.

A local LLM made adverse-event coding hierarchical and reviewable

A second August 18 preprint applies the same transfer idea to safety language: use broad model knowledge, but constrain the output through an established evidence standard. The researchers analyzed 848 unique adverse-event descriptions from three prospective controlled acute myeloid leukemia trials—MOSAIC, DELTA, and DaunoDouble.

MedDRA contains more than 80,000 Low Level Terms. The pipeline first embedded each trial description and retrieved the 100 most semantically similar candidates. A locally run Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct model selected one LLT in a machine-readable format. Deterministic lookup then carried that choice upward to the corresponding Preferred Term and System Organ Class.

This division of labor gives each component a legible role. Retrieval limits the vocabulary, the language model resolves clinical phrasing, and the terminology table preserves the hierarchy. Dense retrieval placed the original coder's LLT inside the top 100 for 88% of MOSAIC descriptions, 76% of DELTA, and 80% of DaunoDouble, outperforming lexical retrieval across all three datasets.

Exact accuracy rose as the hierarchy broadened: 50%-58% at LLT, 78%-85% at PT, and 90%-93% at SOC. Two independent clinicians then reviewed the selected LLTs, with a third resolving disagreements. Their Clinical Correctness Rate was 91%-97%. In 38%-41% of apparent exact-match errors, the model had selected a synonym or near-equivalent term the reviewers considered clinically acceptable.

The study repeated the pipeline across three random seeds and compared five open instruction-tuned backbones. Variation was greatest at the most granular LLT level; the higher PT and SOC levels were more stable. That is useful operational information: a safety workflow can choose where it needs literal label reproducibility and where clinically equivalent coding is sufficient.

The next validation is prospective. The study is retrospective, limited to AML, and does not yet measure coder time, integration effort, or user acceptance. Clinical acceptability is not formal regulatory validation, and transport will need testing across therapeutic areas, languages, institutions, and MedDRA versions. The underlying trial data remain private; the authors say code will be released with publication.

Transfer is becoming an evidence capability

These studies operate at different scales, but they share an architecture. PanoraOnc transfers patterns learned from broad real-world cohorts into a new outcome study. The adverse-event system transfers language understanding into a controlled terminology without giving up deterministic lineage or expert review.

That is a constructive direction for AI-enabled RWE: build models that arrive with useful prior structure, then make their contribution visible inside the scientific workflow. A pretrained representation can still be tested against held-out patients. A model-selected code can still be traced through MedDRA and adjudicated by clinicians. The capability grows, and so does the number of places where evidence teams can inspect it.
