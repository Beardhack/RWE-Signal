The LLM Built a 17,223-Patient Cohort. The Guideline's Middle Still Collapsed.

An LLM-enabled three-system study validated the new pulmonary-embolism categories at the extremes—but not across the intermediate band where many treatment decisions sit

August 11, 2026 — Daily edition

The language model did what large-scale real-world evidence increasingly asks it to do: turn routine clinical text into analyzable variables. The resulting cohort was large. The clinical framework being tested still had to earn its distinctions.

A new preprint used an independently evaluated LLM abstraction framework to help assemble 17,223 confirmed pulmonary-embolism cases across three health systems. Mortality rose sharply from the broad low-risk category to the most acute. Inside the intermediate band, however, the finer subcategories did not separate in the expected order and did not improve overall discrimination.

A second study, also released August 10, found the same boundary from another direction. A large open-source model identified PCI reports with 96.4% accuracy across three hospitals. When asked to classify the harder research phenotype—complex PCI—its positive predictive value fell to 57.6%.

Together, the studies move the useful question beyond whether an LLM can read a note. The consequential test is whether task-specific errors, missing tests, and local documentation differences change the cohort or conclusion built from the model's output.

The cohort scaled. The middle categories did not separate.

Mahmoud Alwakeel and colleagues evaluated the new 2026 AHA/ACC pulmonary-embolism framework in patients drawn from Duke, Stanford's INSPECT cohort, and MIMIC-IV at Beth Israel Deaconess. Duke supplied 12,992 confirmed cases, Stanford 3,870, and MIMIC-IV 361.

The text layer mattered. The team used a previously peer-reviewed LLM framework to extract pulmonary-embolism presence and location, right-heart strain, and study quality from routine CT pulmonary angiography reports, then joined those imaging phenotypes with structured EHR biomarkers, procedures, and mortality.

The broad mortality staircase was real: pooled 30-day mortality was 1.5% in category B, 8.9% in C, 15.5% in D, and 31.9% in E. Each cohort preserved that order. The framework separated the extremes.

The fine-grained middle was less convincing. At Duke, mortality was 9.2% in C1, rose to 10.8% in C2, fell to 8.1% in C3, and returned to 10.9% in D1. Using every subcategory yielded a C-index of 0.696; collapsing the intermediate categories yielded 0.697. More labels did not create more discrimination.

Missingness added another warning. Category-C patients who lacked both echocardiography and biomarkers were not low risk: mortality was 10.4% among 967 such Duke patients and 7.3% among 643 at Stanford. Because missing tests were treated as criteria not met, incomplete evaluation could make an acutely ill patient appear less severe.

The LLM itself was not a neutral pipe. The underlying peer-reviewed abstraction evaluation found strong performance for pulmonary-embolism presence, but materially weaker agreement for right-heart strain—one of the features feeding the risk categories. Extraction error can therefore propagate into the very strata being validated.

The preprint is retrospective, the categories derive from expert consensus, and treatment may flatten observed risk among patients receiving advanced intervention. External cohorts also lacked some echocardiography and procedure data. The result is not that the framework failed everywhere. It is narrower and more useful: the extremes appear informative, while the intermediate distinctions require prospective testing that models missingness and phenotype error explicitly.

A 96.4% accuracy headline hid the harder 57.6% precision problem

Nishi Bhatt and colleagues tested three open-source language models on 1,412 de-identified cardiac-catheterization reports from three hospitals in Yale New Haven Health. Four interventional-cardiology fellows annotated the records, and a board-certified interventional cardiologist adjudicated the reference standard.

Llama 3.3 70B identified PCI reports with 100% sensitivity, 93.8% specificity, and 96.4% accuracy. It made no false-negative PCI calls among 1,386 evaluable reports. The smaller medical models did not show comparable balance: Meditron-7B labeled so many non-PCI reports positive that specificity fell to 7.8%, while BioMistral-7B missed nearly every PCI report.

Complex PCI was the more consequential test for research use. Llama found nearly every true complex case, but it also produced 92 false positives against 125 true positives. Its positive predictive value was 57.6%. A model-positive complex-PCI cohort accepted without review would therefore contain substantial phenotype noise.

The errors were clinically legible. Seventy percent of false-positive PCI identifications involved intravascular ultrasound or fractional-flow-reserve procedures without an intervention. The model also confused two lesions in one vessel with two treated vessels. These are not random text mistakes; they are distinctions that change whether a case meets a research definition.

Performance varied by hospital even within one system, with complex-classification accuracy ranging from 75.6% to 88.2%. The study was retrospective and did not test a live workflow. Data and code could not be released under privacy and IRB restrictions, and accuracy for rare variables benefited from mostly negative labels. External validation is essential before the phenotype is reused across sites.

What changed: extraction accuracy is no longer the finish line

These studies show the AI-RWE intersection becoming concrete. One LLM pipeline helped convert years of radiology reports into a multi-system cohort used to interrogate a new guideline. Another tested whether routine procedure narratives could become a scalable complex-PCI phenotype.

Both also reveal the next standard. A useful model must be evaluated at the level of the claim built from its output. For pulmonary embolism, that means testing whether uncertain imaging abstractions and missing exams change risk strata. For complex PCI, it means measuring how false-positive phenotypes alter cohort composition and downstream effect estimates.

The credible deployment pattern remains task-specific: preserve an audit trail, expose missingness, review the positive or uncertain cases that matter, and validate the final research conclusion across sites. A large cohort and a high accuracy number are valuable inputs. Neither is the endpoint.
