An LLM Pipeline Turned Hospital Timelines Into Causal Models

Three preprints put language models inside causal estimation, clinical-study retrieval, and transparent protocol-compliance analysis.

RWE-Signal  ·  August 17, 2026  ·  Daily edition

The most consequential new work in AI-enabled evidence is happening between the raw record and the final estimate. New systems are assigning language models bounded jobs inside larger scientific structures: mapping patient timelines into causal graphs, retrieving trials against expert reference sets, and resolving clinical vocabulary before deterministic rules measure care.

The lead development is expert-guided g-computation, an August 11 preprint that uses an LLM to scale the patient-specific reasoning needed to estimate the likely effect of hospital interventions that have not yet been implemented. It is a direct attempt to use unstructured real-world records where standard causal designs have little or no exposure support.

Patient timelines became intervention-specific causal graphs

The UCSF-led team begins with a difficult quality-improvement question: how much time would a proposed intervention save when the hospital has never deployed it? Historical data cannot identify every relationship, while a single population-level causal graph can flatten the different event structures carried by individual patients.

The proposed answer, called egg-computation, joins clinical judgment with formal g-computation. An LLM reads notes, orders, test results, and intervention definitions, then builds a patient-specific Gantt chart with causal semantics. Statistical timing models handle relationships supported by observed data; expert reasoning is reserved for the intervention mechanism and other parts that data alone cannot identify.

The real-world evaluation covered eleven proposed interventions at an urban safety-net hospital. Five quality-improvement experts reviewed graphs for early discharge planning and disposition-critical imaging. Across those tasks, intervention-target specificity was 96.5%, recall ranged from 88.9% to 95.7%, edge precision from 91.8% to 94.2%, and edge recall from 80.2% to 86.0%. Nineteen of the 24 reviewed pairs produced identical time-saved estimates before and after expert correction.

On 4,790 downstream event timings, GPT-5 in-context learning reached a mean absolute error of 24.12 hours, improving on the closest pair-type-median baseline at 27.23 hours. Clinicians then reviewed intervention summaries and observed-versus-counterfactual timelines, and found the causal ordering more plausible than ranking projects only by how many patients appeared eligible.

The estimates remain prioritization evidence, not proof of realized operational effects. Only 24 graph pairs received expert review, and the current method does not model competition for shared resources across patients; under constrained capacity, an estimated effect can behave like an upper bound. The engineering opportunity is now clear: add resource interference, formal uncertainty for expert inputs, and prospective evaluation after an intervention launches.

Cochrane reviews became a benchmark for chatbot evidence retrieval

An NIH-led team asked a complementary question: when a web-enabled chatbot cites clinical studies, does it find the same primary evidence an expert review included? The August 13 preprint tested Claude Sonnet 5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and ChatGPT GPT-5.5 on 20 intervention questions from current Cochrane reviews.

Each system answered as a patient, clinician, and evidence-synthesis researcher, with four independent runs per combination. That produced 720 responses whose citations could be compared with the reviews' included and excluded study sets. Across all runs, one response retrieved a mean 39.2% of included studies and 5.0% of excluded studies.

After controlling for year, citation rate, and open-access status, sample size was the only independently significant predictor of retrieval: the odds increased 1.80-fold for each one-unit rise in log sample size. That finding turns a vague concern about search behavior into a testable selection property.

The team's public repository preserves unchanged model responses, review-specific analyses, curated artifacts, figures, and a demo. Licensed Cochrane source packages are not redistributed, so complete regeneration still requires authorized access. The benchmark also measures retrieval rather than downstream synthesis quality. Its lasting value may be the evaluation pattern: modern evidence agents can be tested against expert study sets, by task framing, before their selections enter a review.

Neuro-symbolic design separated language from sepsis evidence logic

A third preprint uses a smaller local model for a narrower but reusable RWD task. In 2,438 MIMIC-IV sepsis episodes, MedGemma-4b-it normalized the trade names, abbreviations, and microbiology strings that make longitudinal protocol analysis difficult. It did not score care.

Explicit drug rules verified model classifications, an embedding check confirmed 210 of 213 non-other classifications, and unresolved cases went to expert review. A deterministic fuzzy-inference layer then applied eight Surviving Sepsis Campaign rules, replacing hard timing cutoffs with graded functions whose boundaries were set with domain expertise.

Under the study's uniform one-hour operationalization, antibiotic timing recorded the lowest mean score at 0.24, with 13% of episodes meeting the threshold. The authors also report median ICU stays of 3.8 days in the high-compliance group and 5.1 days in the low-compliance group. The observed overall correlation was weak, so these are descriptive signals rather than a causal treatment-effect estimate.

The design is more important than any single compliance number. The onset anchor can penalize antibiotics delivered before formal ICU onset, missing documentation is counted conservatively, mortality was not studied, and expert-set fuzzy boundaries require calibration. Yet the modular architecture makes those assumptions visible. The next version can credit pre-ICU care, calibrate boundaries against outcomes, and transport the same semantic-to-symbolic pattern to other protocol-driven RWD studies.

The new signal is division of scientific labor

Today's three sources do not ask one model to own the answer. They give language models specific evidence jobs: infer the structure of a patient timeline, retrieve studies for comparison with an expert set, or translate clinical vocabulary into a controlled representation.

Formal estimands, reference corpora, deterministic rules, and expert review then carry the authority. That division of labor is a constructive path for AI-enabled RWE: broader reach through models, stronger inspectability through the surrounding evidence system.
