The AI Cut Estimated Review Time. It Also Missed 49% of the Search.

Three peer-reviewed validations found useful automation at the shallow end of evidence work—and brittle performance where completeness and clinical interpretation matter most

August 12, 2026 — Daily edition

The labor-saving estimates deserve attention. So do the omissions.

An AI-assisted replication of a health-economic systematic review estimated savings of up to 60% for search and title-and-abstract screening. Its generated search retrieved only 22 of the 43 PubMed articles in the original review. Accuracy exceeded 95% on titles and abstracts, then fell below 80% when the system reached full text and the modeling choices became harder to interpret.

Two other peer-reviewed studies reached the same boundary in different evidence workflows. GPT-4o extracted elements from pharmacovigilance case narratives with F1 scores ranging from 0.429 to 1.000, depending on the element. A fine-tuned oncology model turned more than two million clinical notes into 2.5 million staging records, but its ground truth came from 700 notes within the same health system.

The shared signal is not that the tools lack value. It is that the value concentrates in ranking, structuring, and accelerating work, while the errors concentrate where missed evidence or misunderstood context can change the conclusion.

The fastest search retrieved only half the benchmark articles

Lisa Bloudek and colleagues reported the validation July 29 in PharmacoEconomics. The team replicated a previously completed manual review of economic evaluations for chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps using two components in the Nested Knowledge platform: a machine-learning inclusion predictor and an LLM-based criteria screener.

The top-line efficiency result was attractive. Estimated savings reached roughly 60% for searches and title-and-abstract screening and about 20% for data extraction, depending on how much human validation each task still required.

The completeness result changes how that efficiency should be read. The generated search found 51% of the PubMed articles included in the original review. For evidence synthesis, a missed article is not simply work avoided. It can remove a comparator, cost input, utility estimate, or modeling precedent from the evidence base.

The decline continued as the task became more interpretive. The system performed reliably on broad study descriptors, but struggled with model structures, health states, outcomes, the distinction between sensitivity and scenario analyses, and assumptions governing duration of response, discontinuation, surgery, and mortality.

Those are not formatting details. They are the choices that determine how a health-economic model behaves. The study therefore supports AI as a prioritization and extraction aid while arguing against treating a fast review as a complete one.

The commercial context matters: three authors were affiliated with Nested Knowledge, whose platform was evaluated, and two with Curta. The accessible primary abstract describes one review in one disease area, and the publisher's full text requires a subscription. Independent replication across different economic models would show whether the miss rate is a property of this corpus, this implementation, or the task itself.

Safety extraction ranged from near failure to a perfect score

A July 29 study in Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics tested GPT-4o inside a proprietary pharmacovigilance platform. Jeffrey Warner and colleagues asked the system to extract risk factors and responses to dechallenge and rechallenge from individual case safety report narratives across five historical signal assessments.

The range is more informative than a single average. At its best, the system extracted an element perfectly. At its weakest, it fell below F1 0.45. Dechallenge and rechallenge are particularly consequential because they help reviewers assess whether an adverse event changes when exposure stops or resumes.

The authors retained a machine-first, human-verified workflow and reported potential time savings even after verification. That is the credible operational claim. The accessible abstract does not supply the exact case count, task prevalence, or quantitative saving, so it cannot establish how much review could safely be removed.

The feasibility study was retrospective, covered five historical assessments, and used a proprietary platform. All authors were affiliated with Eli Lilly. Before such a system changes staffing or review depth, it needs external testing that reports false negatives by case element and measures the human work required to find them.

Two million notes became 2.5 million staging records

The scale story comes from oncology. In a July 13 report in JCO Clinical Cancer Informatics, Swapna Abhyankar and colleagues fine-tuned a language model to extract overall cancer stage, T, N, M, timeframe, and associated diagnosis from routine clinical documentation.

The ground-truth set contained 700 notes. The trained pipeline then processed more than two million notes and generated more than 2.5 million records for 217,768 patients. Relation-level precision across the six extracted attributes ranged from 0.77 to 1.0 in the first three cancers and from 0.83 to 1.0 in breast and prostate without additional training.

That is a real gain in RWD availability. Stage is central to oncology cohort definition, prognosis, treatment comparison, and adjustment. Information that was inaccessible in narrative form became queryable at patient scale.

Scale is not transportability. The labeled notes and the millions of generated records came from one integrated health system. The abstract reports precision, not the recall, patient-level consistency, subgroup behavior, or downstream effect of errors. Evaluation at three additional systems was underway, not complete.

All authors were affiliated with Truveta, and the model was TLM-Oncology. The next evidence threshold is therefore external and consequential: show how the phenotype travels across documentation systems and whether its errors change a cohort or treatment-effect estimate.

What changed: validation has to follow the decision

These reports make a coherent case for AI inside evidence workflows. It can rank papers, propose extractions, surface facts from safety narratives, and convert clinical text into structured variables at a scale manual review cannot match.

They also show why an aggregate accuracy or time-saving number is an incomplete endpoint. Search recall determines which evidence exists for the reviewer. Interpretation determines whether an economic model is represented correctly. Element-level extraction determines whether a safety narrative supports a causal signal. Phenotype transportability determines whether a staging variable means the same thing at another site.

The practical standard is to validate the consequence, not only the component. Measure whether the missed paper changes the review, whether the missed safety detail changes the assessment, and whether the staging error changes the estimate. Until then, efficiency is a workflow result—not an evidence result.
