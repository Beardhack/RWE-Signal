FedEHR-Agents Let Hospitals Share Modeling Intelligence Without Pooling Patient Records

A new multi-hospital EHR benchmark shifts federation from model weights to agent experience—and posts gains on mortality, respiratory failure, length of stay, and sepsis.

RWE-Signal  ·  August 31, 2026  ·  Monday edition

The craft of building a clinical model usually stays trapped inside one institution: how its data were cleaned, which features survived, what failed in validation, and how the pipeline improved. Federated learning can move model updates without moving raw records, but it rarely transfers that wider modeling intelligence.

FedEHR-Agents, posted August 28 by researchers at McGill University and Mila, makes the modeling process itself collaborative. Hospital agents work on local longitudinal EHRs, refine their own preprocessing and model-development playbooks, and contribute evidence-backed experience to a federation. Across four clinical prediction tasks, that shared experience outperformed the same agents working alone.

The shared object is now the modeling playbook

Each participating hospital runs an autonomous clinical agent with two working modules. One transforms local EHR tables into task-specific features; the other selects, trains, and evaluates prediction models. Historical memory, task-specific evaluators, execution feedback, and TextGrad prompt refinement let the agent improve its local workflow over repeated rounds.

The server does more than average those prompts. Its evidence-guided aggregation weighs the associated validation performance, feature statistics, feature importance, model choices, and optimization trends, preserves complementary strategies, and synthesizes global meta-prompts. Those prompts return to each hospital for the next local round.

That is the paper's central design move. The collaboration is about how an agent builds an EHR model—its executable, testable playbook—while each institution can keep a different local prediction model suited to its data.

Evidence-guided agents improved all four prediction tasks

The authors evaluate the framework on the public eICU Collaborative Research Database, treating hospitals as separate federated clients. The main experiment uses three hospitals, five communication rounds, and three random seeds. Agents tackle four outcomes with different horizons: mortality within 48 hours, acute respiratory failure within four hours, length of stay beyond seven days, and sepsis.

FedEHR-Agents produced the best result among the decentralized methods on every task. The AUPRC gains over a local agent ranged from 8.3% for mortality to 17.9% for sepsis. It also beat a simpler prompt-averaging federation across all four outcomes, which matters because it isolates the value of pairing prompts with structured execution evidence.

The ablation study reinforces that point. Removing evidence-guided aggregation reduced mortality AUPRC from 0.236 to 0.221; removing the autonomous data-preprocessing module produced an even larger drop, to 0.219. Memory and evaluator feedback also contributed. In this benchmark, better federation did not replace the local workflow—it amplified a full agent stack.

The advantage grew with a larger federation

The experiment then expands the simulated federation from three hospitals to 20. Performance rises on every task, and evidence-guided experience aggregation retains its advantage over simple prompt averaging at each tested scale. The framework also beats the prompt-averaging variant when GPT-4o, GPT-5 mini, or GPT-5 is used as the underlying model.

The most encouraging signal is not simply that more simulated hospitals helped. It is that their heterogeneous experience became a useful collaborative object: feature selection and SHAP-based importance grew more consistent across agents, while hospital-specific distributions remained distinct.

The boundary is equally clear. This is a non-peer-reviewed, retrospective experiment on standardized partitions of one public critical-care database, not a live federation among health systems. Refined prompts and modeling statistics can still encode local data characteristics, and the paper does not claim differential privacy, secure aggregation, or protection from reconstruction attacks. Those are the next engineering layer, alongside prospective validation and long-term monitoring.

A new unit of exchange for evidence engineering

Today's genuinely new signal is a shift in what distributed health-AI systems can learn together. FedEHR-Agents shows that the reusable asset may be the evidence-backed process behind a model—how agents preprocess records, select features, respond to evaluation, and refine their decisions—rather than only the resulting parameters.

That opens a constructive path for AI-enabled evidence work across institutions: keep patient records and local accountability close to the source, make agent decisions measurable, and share only the experience that can be evaluated and governed. Formal privacy controls and real-world deployment tests are now the practical route from a strong benchmark to trustworthy infrastructure.
