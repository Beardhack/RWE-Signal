RWE Automation Meets the Protocol Test

A new open-source target-trial workflow makes advanced causal analyses easier to run, while FDA's 2025 ledger and August research show why data fit and diagnostic discipline remain the hard part

RWE-Signal — August 4, 2026 — Daily edition

Real-world evidence is becoming easier to compute and harder to excuse. The newest development is practical: statistician Hisashi Noma has released a detailed tutorial and public R package that turn much of target-trial emulation's technical machinery into a reproducible workflow. It arrives as FDA's current accounting shows a much larger RWE regulatory pipeline, and as new research makes patient-level data continuity measurable rather than merely aspirational.

The pieces point in the same direction. Better tooling can standardize implementation. Linkage can widen the observable record. Agents can coordinate specialized work. But the scientific burden remains attached to decisions about time zero, treatment strategies, cohort fit, missing care, outcome definitions, and the interpretation of a result. Automation is moving downstream of those decisions faster than it is resolving them.

That distinction matters now because RWE is no longer a niche supplement. FDA recorded three times as many protocols containing real-world data in fiscal 2025 as in 2024. The question is shifting from whether these methods will enter mainstream evidence workflows to whether their assumptions, transformations, and failure modes can remain visible when they do.

The newest target-trial toolkit packages the mechanics, not the judgment

Noma submitted Target Trial Emulation with the R Package TTE on August 3. The tutorial covers target-trial protocols, intention-to-treat and per-protocol estimands, baseline and person-period data, treatment and censoring weights, truncation, balance and effective-sample-size diagnostics, pooled discrete-time survival models, standardization, competing risks, weighted survival curves, and individual-cluster bootstrap.

The accompanying TTE 1.1.1 repository and worked scripts make the contribution more than a methods overview. One synthetic example compares initiation of SGLT2 and DPP-4 inhibitors with all-cause death; another builds sequentially nested trials comparing angiotensin receptor blockers with calcium channel blockers, treating death as a competing event for heart-failure hospitalization. The repository exposes the workflow's intermediate pieces—data checks, weights, diagnostics, outcome models, standardization, and curves—rather than hiding them behind a single command.

The restraint in the documentation is the important part. TTE does not claim to identify a causal effect automatically. Users still have to defend eligibility criteria, treatment strategies, assignment procedures, time zero, grace periods, outcomes, confounders, estimands, positivity, exchangeability, measurement timing, and missingness. That is not a disclaimer around the method; it is the method.

The stakes are visible in a May BMJ systematic review of 107 target-trial-emulation and randomized-trial pairs. Seventy-nine percent met its standardized-difference agreement measure and 77% met its estimate-agreement measure, but the overall Pearson correlation was a more modest 0.59. Seventy-four percent of emulated estimates were within 20% of the paired trial result. Concordance weakened when investigators could not closely align populations, treatment timing, or outcome measurement. The new package lowers implementation friction; it does not make those alignment problems disappear.

This is still early evidence. Noma's article is a single-author preprint and both tutorials use synthetic data. Its immediate value is therefore reproducibility and education—not proof that analyses conducted with the package will agree with randomized trials.

FDA's RWE pipeline grew faster than its success count

FDA's current CDER ledger reports 31 protocols containing real-world data in fiscal 2025, up from 11 in 2024 and 10 in 2023. The mix was nearly even between effectiveness and safety: 15 and 16 protocols, respectively. Twenty-five were non-interventional studies, five were externally controlled trials, and one was randomized.

The application count also jumped. FDA records ten NDA or BLA submissions containing RWE for fiscal 2025, compared with one in 2024. Four were approvals in which RWE contributed to the agency's decision: Acetadote, Onapgo, Isturisa, and Gamifant. One additional approval included RWE that did not contribute. The remaining outcomes were four complete responses and one refusal to file.

Those numbers are evidence of activity, not a conversion rate. FDA says the report does not capture every CDER submission containing RWD analyses, and a study can appear in more than one data-source or purpose category. It also does not reveal why an application failed or how much weight RWE carried inside an approval. Even so, the distance between 31 protocols, ten applications, and four contributing approvals is a useful picture of expanding activity alongside selective decisions.

The source mix is broadening with the volume. Among the 31 protocols, FDA counted 11 using EHR data, seven using claims, and 12 using registries; categories can overlap. The rise of externally controlled trials and effectiveness-focused protocols makes data provenance, endpoint capture, comparability, and prespecification more consequential, not less.

Linkage is turning observability into a measurable study variable

A paper in the August issue of JAMIA Open asks a question that should precede many EHR analyses: how much of each patient's care can the study actually see? Yao An Lee and colleagues linked OneFlorida+ EHR records to Florida Medicaid claims, then externally validated their approach with REACHnet EHRs linked to Louisiana Blue claims.

The linked claims served as a reference for care occurring beyond the indexed EHR. As the authors' Harmonized Encounter Proportion Score increased, misclassification fell across 42 conditions and medications. In the development network, a score near 30% was the point at which standardized differences generally fell below the study's prespecified threshold. An XGBoost model using routinely collected EHR features predicted high continuity with an AUROC of 0.77.

The equity analysis complicates any impulse to keep only the most observable patients. Before mitigation, the complete-predictor model's false-negative rate was 0.60 for Hispanic patients and 0.35 for non-Hispanic White patients. After adversarial debiasing, those rates moved to 0.46 and 0.50. The authors selected false negatives as the fairness target because incorrectly labeling a truly high-continuity patient as low continuity can exclude that person from research. The trade-off is fundamental: selecting for data completeness can improve measurement while changing who remains represented.

An applied oncology study in the same August publication cycle shows why the measurement layer matters. B. N. Cajiao Garcia and colleagues linked the Netherlands Cancer Registry with nationwide pathology reports to study RET-fusion testing in metastatic non-squamous lung cancer. Testing rose from 22.6% in 2017 to 31.8% in 2019, while practice shifted sharply from FISH toward RNA-based methods. Observed RET-fusion prevalence fell from 1.3% to 0.7%, leading the authors to argue that the often-cited 1–2% prevalence may be high.

The cohorts are historical and Dutch, so that prevalence should not be transported casually. The broader lesson is durable: when the testing process changes, the measured population can change with it. Linkage expands the field of view; it does not by itself prove that exposures, covariates, or endpoints are complete enough for the intended question.

Agentic RWE is becoming a claim about the entire evidence lifecycle

The EmulatRx system published in Nature Communications on July 7 offers the clearest recent example of what an agentic evidence workflow can look like. A Supervisor coordinates Trialist, Informatician, Clinician, and Statistician agents across trial retrieval, protocol parsing, cohort construction, causal analysis, and review. The system stores transformations and conversation state, constrains handoffs with a graph, and fixes seeds for downstream statistical tools.

The authors evaluated 20 clinical trials across MIMIC-IV and the INSIGHT network. On 266 manually annotated eligibility concepts, GPT-4o reached 98.9% recall and 96.7% precision. That is a concrete result for protocol parsing, but it is not an end-to-end validation of autonomous RWE. Some causal-effect tests used synthetic cohorts of 1,000 subjects, and the clinician agent was assessed by three domain experts. The paper demonstrates a research architecture and task performance; it does not establish that the system can independently produce regulatory-grade studies.

The research community is beginning to draw that same boundary. An active Journal of Biomedical Informatics special issue led by Richard Boyce, Scott Alexander Malec, and Chunhua Weng asks for work across data harmonization, phenotyping, protocol specification, causal critique, target-trial emulation, provenance, monitoring, governance, and safe deployment. It explicitly places isolated tool-augmented agents outside scope.

A call for papers is an agenda, not evidence. But the agenda is telling: the unit of evaluation is moving from whether an agent can write code or retrieve a cohort to whether a multi-step evidence process can remain reproducible, reviewable, and accountable.

What changed: the machinery is becoming inspectable

The genuinely new signal is that a difficult causal workflow now has a public, runnable implementation guide that exposes its diagnostics and assumptions. FDA's fiscal 2025 record supplies the institutional backdrop: RWE activity is scaling quickly enough that consistency and inspection cannot depend on artisanal review alone.

The recurring theme is that connectivity and computation are not the same as evidence fitness. The data-continuity study measures how much care is visible and how selection can affect equity. The RET study shows how testing practice changes the apparent prevalence of a biomarker. EmulatRx shows how agents can divide the labor while also revealing how much of their evaluation remains task-specific or synthetic.

RWE automation is therefore crossing an important threshold, but not the one described by most product claims. The advance is not autonomous truth. It is a more inspectable production line—one that can make protocol decisions, data gaps, model behavior, and analytic diagnostics easier to see, if teams choose to preserve them.
