A Longitudinal Foundation Model Learned From 1.67 Million Cancer Journeys

New systems turn patient trajectories, guideline criteria, and trial registrations into explicit objects for AI-enabled evidence work.

RWE-Signal  ·  August 27, 2026  ·  Daily edition

Three new releases show frontier AI moving deeper into the structure of clinical evidence. A longitudinal oncology foundation model learns reusable patient-state representations from clinical and molecular histories; retrieval-augmented agents apply expert-defined criteria to clinical-practice guidelines; and an LLM restores missing links between trial registrations and result papers.

The common advance is an explicit evidence object. Each system gives the model something durable to represent, appraise, or connect, then measures its work against curated features, human judgments, or registered studies. That architecture creates a practical route to scale without making provenance or review disappear.

oFM represented cancer as a changing patient state

The oFM living preprint, posted August 25, integrates longitudinal clinical records with DNA, RNA, and H&E pathology from 1.67 million people with cancer. More than one million patient journeys trained the model, with patient-level partitions reserved for validation and testing.

Instead of freezing a patient into one feature table, oFM encodes clinical and molecular events into daily episodes and carries them through a temporal transformer. The resulting patient-state embedding can be reused for response, survival, treatment-benefit, and mechanism-discovery work.

For overall survival, frozen oFM embeddings reached an AUC of 0.774, compared with 0.563 for expert-curated baseline features. In 11 comparative-treatment cohorts, the model produced stronger treatment-benefit ranking in nine; its pooled scale-normalized AUTOC statistic was 4.61, versus 1.38 for the baseline. The authors also connect model predictions to attributed clinical and molecular events through an evidence-grounded temporal graph.

This is a vendor-authored living preprint built on retrospective proprietary data. The authors expressly frame the results as tests of representation quality, not validated causal treatment effects, biomarkers, or clinical deployment. Its opportunity is the reusable substrate: one longitudinal representation can support multiple evidence questions while keeping downstream validation task-specific.

Structured guidance strengthened guideline-appraisal agents

A peer-reviewed JMIR study published August 26 tested whether the same expert-developed workbook that improved human consistency could also improve retrieval-augmented AI agents. The agents used DeepSeek-R1 or o1-mini to appraise 227 rehabilitation guidelines with AGREE II and RIGHT criteria.

The workbook raised agent-human agreement for both models. DeepSeek-R1's intraclass correlation increased from 0.613 to 0.709; o1-mini's rose from 0.629 to 0.687. In an external set of six ACL-reconstruction guidelines, DeepSeek-R1 maintained an ICC of 0.711 and completed each appraisal in a mean 5.44 minutes, compared with 11.18 minutes for humans.

The result makes expert guidance a first-class technical component, not just a prompt accessory. The agent retrieves the relevant appraisal criterion before producing a score, confidence rating, and written justification, leaving methodologists a structured object to verify.

The six-guideline external set is small, and agreement inside a shared rubric is not independent truth. The agents sometimes supplied unsupported references or overinterpreted ambiguous text. Those boundaries point directly to the responsible scaling path: broader specialty validation, preserved justifications, and human resolution of methodological ambiguity.

TrialScout restored provenance across 9,600 trials

TrialScout's peer-reviewed article appeared electronically August 26 after an earlier public preprint. The tool uses an LLM to decide whether a PubMed paper reports results from a ClinicalTrials.gov registration, addressing a persistent break in the evidence chain when publications do not carry an explicit registry link.

Against prior human coding, TrialScout reached 92.5% sensitivity and 81.2% specificity. The most revealing validation step reviewed 200 disagreements: 123, or 61.5%, were attributable to human errors. Applied to a random sample of 9,600 completed or terminated trials, the system found result publications for 6,110, or 63.6%.

The imperfect human reference means there is no error-free gold standard, but the manual disagreement audit is itself a positive contribution. It shows how AI can help evidence-synthesis and transparency teams inspect both missing links and the assumptions in their reference data. The next unlock is an openly maintained adjudication set that can measure improvements without treating either human or model decisions as automatically final.

The new signal is evidence made explicit

Today's releases span different stages of the lifecycle, but they converge on the same systems choice. oFM makes a longitudinal patient state reusable. The guideline agents make appraisal criteria retrievable and reviewable. TrialScout makes the relationship between a registration and its published result machine-checkable.

That is a constructive path for AI-enabled RWE: build flexible reasoning around stable evidence objects, benchmark each handoff, and preserve the human checkpoints that make the result credible. As those objects become more interoperable, agents can move faster across the evidence lifecycle without erasing where an answer came from.
