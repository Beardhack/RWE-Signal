The Agent Ran the Study. Humans Rebuilt the Cohort.

A governed AI orchestrated a 286,422-person hospital GWAS without receiving patient rows; human review still had to decide who counted as hypertensive

August 9, 2026 — Daily edition

The agent planned the analysis, launched the pipeline, checked its own artifacts, and reproduced known genetic associations. First, however, it built the wrong hypertension cohort.

That tension defines the most consequential new evidence at the intersection of AI agents and real-world data. In a July 13 preprint, a governed research system worked across a hospital-linked genotype and EHR warehouse without receiving raw patient rows. Its initial phenotype classified 3,950 people as cases whom the expert definition treated as controls. A human-led medication audit and directed reconciliation were needed before the results aligned with the expert analysis.

Two other studies sharpen the boundary. An agent team generated and repaired complete machine-learning pipelines across MIMIC-IV and a longitudinal rheumatoid-arthritis cohort, but its most difficult real-world task remained modest and its feature review relied partly on another model. A peer-reviewed three-site study moved small adapter weights instead of clinical notes and nearly matched pooled training, while warning that the weights themselves had not been audited for privacy leakage.

The emerging pattern is not autonomous evidence generation. It is governed execution with visible failure points: humans still own the phenotype, the external validation, and the privacy claims that the architecture cannot prove by itself.

The agent had controlled access. It did not have scientific authority.

Eddie Huang and colleagues described the NVAITC AI Scientist, a governed agent system that translated a hypertension research question into cohort extraction, quality control, PLINK2 analysis, and manuscript artifacts. The case study used 286,422 people in a hospital-linked genotype and EHR environment.

The access design was deliberately narrow. Raw protected health information stayed inside the authorized warehouse. The agent received aggregate results such as row counts, summaries, plots, and manifests; external network access was blocked by default. Human approval gates controlled sensitive steps.

The governance worked as an access control. The phenotype still failed as a research judgment. The expert definition found 109,782 cases and 176,640 controls. An early agent definition incorporated blood-pressure measurements and produced 3,950 agent-case/expert-control disagreements.

Of those 3,950 people, 2,911 had a recorded blood pressure above 140/90, but only 125 had an antihypertensive prescription. The paper's central scientific repair was not another autonomous pass. Investigators audited medication evidence and directed the system to reconcile its rule with the intended phenotype.

After reconciliation, the workflow reproduced established loci including FGF5, ATP2B1, CNNM2, FTO, and GRB14. That is a replication result, not a claim of a new association. The agent also caught a prior PLINK label-encoding artifact that had silently excluded every sample, showing real value in artifact inspection and reproducibility checks.

The limits are substantial. This is a preprint, one institutional data environment, and the endpoint was reached after months of capability engineering by a team that included NVIDIA's AI Technology Center and hospital researchers. The study demonstrates governed orchestration and error recovery. It does not show that an agent can independently choose a defensible phenotype.

A team of agents built the pipeline. The hardest cohort stayed hard.

Ruilin Wang and colleagues reported DoctorAgents on August 5. The framework assigns separate agents to consult on the clinical task, generate code, repair failures, log experience, evaluate results, and optimize the next pipeline. A domain-specific version divides data preprocessing from model development.

The authors tested three MIMIC-IV tasks—mortality, seven-day readmission, and length of stay longer than three days—plus longitudinal records from an Early Undifferentiated PolyArthritis cohort, where the target was response to anti-TNF treatment after one to nine years of observation.

The domain-specific system led the reported comparator on all four tasks. The margins were small, and the rheumatoid-arthritis result remained weak in absolute terms: an AUROC of 0.577 versus 0.564 for the strongest comparator. Removing the agents' shared memory cut readmission validation AUPRC from 0.255 to 0.215, suggesting that accumulated feedback contributed more than repeated code generation alone.

The evaluation also embeds a caution about automated review. GPT-5 powered the agent frameworks, while GPT-5.4 judged whether generated features were clinically reasonable. The authors acknowledge that model-based judging is imperfect. The preprint used internal five-fold meta-testing, a small longitudinal cohort, and no external institution. It shows that agents can iteratively assemble useful pipelines; it does not establish transportability or clinical validity.

Moving adapter weights kept notes local. Privacy still needed a test.

A peer-reviewed JAMIA Open study by Xingyi Liu and colleagues, published July 20, evaluated sequential fine-tuning across three institutions. The task was to extract mobility functional status and classify impairment from 600 real clinical notes containing 3,810 labeled sections.

Each institution trained a Llama 3.1 8B model with a low-rank adapter, then transferred the adapter weights to the next site. Raw notes stayed local. Sequential training reached an F1 of 0.861 for mobility extraction versus 0.894 for centralized joint training; impairment classification reached 0.912 versus 0.900. At patient level, sequential performance was 0.914 and 0.943.

The result also argues against treating model scale as a substitute for adaptation. The jointly fine-tuned 8B model reached 0.894 and 0.900 on the two tasks, compared with 0.661 and 0.796 for an untuned 70B model. Task-specific learning on real notes mattered more than raw parameter count.

Errors clustered around exactly the context evidence users would need to audit: falls and wheelchair mentions overlapped semantically, test scores such as FOTO and TUG were difficult, and treatment goals were sometimes mistaken for current status. The three sites were in one region, site order was fixed, and the training set was modest.

Most importantly, locality was not presented as proof of privacy. The authors warn that transferred parameters can leak information through inversion or membership-inference attacks. They call for formal privacy audits, secure aggregation, or differential privacy. A distributed architecture reduces raw-data movement; the resulting model updates remain research objects that need their own disclosure test.

What changed: controlled execution is not scientific authority

Today's evidence places AI agents in a narrower and more useful role. They can orchestrate complex workflows, recover from technical failures, generate and repair pipelines, and carry learned parameters between data holders. Those are meaningful capabilities.

They do not erase the decisions that make real-world evidence credible. A human still has to specify what counts as hypertension, decide whether a weak treatment-response model is fit for use, and test whether a supposedly privacy-preserving artifact can reveal its training data.

The practical standard is becoming clearer: constrain access, preserve every executable artifact, expose cohort disagreements before outcome analysis, validate at another institution, and treat both outputs and model updates as potential disclosure surfaces. Governance can define the boundary. It cannot supply the scientific judgment inside it.
