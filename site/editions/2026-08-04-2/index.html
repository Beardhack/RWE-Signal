A Clean Balance Can Hide a Broken Trial

Two July target-trial studies expose confounding that survived conventional diagnostics and deep-learning adjustment, while new agentic tools show why guardrails—not autonomy—are the useful frontier

RWE-Signal — August 4, 2026 — Second edition

The most consequential RWE result in this edition is an answer that changed sign. In the same intensive-care cohort, one reasonable definition of fluid exposure suggested benefit and another suggested harm. The propensity-score diagnostics looked clean in both. The underlying clinical workflow did not.

A second target-trial study reaches the problem from another direction. Four adjustment strategies—including a Transformer—failed to reproduce known randomized benchmarks in heart failure, even though the methods recovered the right answers when all confounders were made observable in simulation.

Together, these studies sharpen the standard for RWE automation. The valuable agent is not the one that produces the most fluent analysis. It is the one constrained by a protocol, deterministic tools, sensitivity tests, negative controls, inspectable artifacts, and an accountable human who can refuse to certify the result.

The diagnostics passed. The answer still flipped.

Qingxia Dai and colleagues published a diagnostic target-trial study in iScience on July 7. They compared balanced crystalloids with saline in MIMIC-IV and pre-specified two exposure definitions: the first fluid given and the fluid that dominated the first 48 hours.

In the same 42,883-patient 48-hour cohort, initial assignment produced a MAKE-30 odds ratio of 0.49, suggesting benefit from balanced fluid. The 48-hour dominant strategy produced an odds ratio of 2.51, suggesting harm. Both were far from the roughly 0.90 benchmark established by the SMART randomized trial.

The familiar checks did not warn investigators away. Overlap weighting reduced the maximum standardized mean difference across 23 baseline covariates to 0.048. The more revealing number was the 89.4% switching rate between first and dominant fluid, consistent with a local workflow in which saline was used during acute resuscitation and balanced fluid later. Baseline balance could not close the post-baseline paths created by evolving illness, treatment switching, survival in the ICU, and institutional practice.

The authors pre-specified pressure ulcers and urinary-tract infections as negative controls. Those outcomes also moved in opposite directions under the two exposure definitions. The pattern disappeared in eICU-CRD, where recorded switching was 1.2%, although variable infusion documentation makes that comparison imperfect.

This is a methods case study, not new clinical evidence about fluids. Its practical result is diagnostic: operationalization sensitivity, switching rates, negative controls, causal diagrams, and cross-database comparison belong alongside balance and positivity checks whenever treatment changes over time.

A deeper model did not recover the randomized result

A July 13 Nature Communications study by Zhengxian Fan and colleagues tested whether more advanced adjustment could solve a related problem: confounding by indication in heart-failure data from CPRD Aurum.

The investigators chose beta-blockers as a positive control and digoxin as a negative control, then applied propensity-score matching, inverse probability weighting, targeted maximum likelihood estimation, and a Transformer-based method. None reproduced the randomized benchmarks. Every method suggested a neutral or harmful beta-blocker effect and higher mortality with digoxin.

The semi-synthetic test is what makes the failure informative. When the same methods were given data in which the relevant confounders were observed, they recovered the true effects. In the real-world analysis, greater flexibility did not manufacture the missing clinical information needed to make treated and untreated patients comparable.

The result should not be generalized into a verdict against target-trial emulation or causal machine learning. It is one disease area and one data source. But positive and negative controls locate the failure more precisely than a model leaderboard could: estimator sophistication cannot compensate for a comparison whose decisive confounding remains unmeasured.

The better agent is the one that cannot improvise

Two new systems show a more credible role for frontier AI in evidence work. Winston Chou and colleagues' July 24 oci-agent preprint divides responsibility among a human principal, an actor that executes the analysis, and a critic that reviews diagnostics. The statistical work runs in templated, re-executable notebooks built on established libraries; the language model is not asked to invent the estimator.

Across 231 ACIC datasets, the critic labeled 192 estimates satisfactory and 39 unsatisfactory; the satisfactory group had lower error and better-calibrated intervals. In a selected ten-dataset ablation, prompt-only output was uncorrelated with ground truth, while the full scaffold recovered nine of ten. On disguised LaLonde tests, the system still posted a 52% median absolute percentage error on the harder PSID comparison—and, importantly, withheld certification in 19 of 20 repetitions because overlap was poor.

The package is a builder-authored preprint whose applied cases come from Netflix, not healthcare. It therefore does not validate autonomous RWE. What it does validate is an architecture in which the agent exposes estimand changes, diagnostics, code, and reasons to stop.

THESEUS, published in JAMIA Open on July 9, draws a similar boundary closer to RWE. An LLM maps a natural-language target-trial description into a reviewable JSON schema; deterministic rules then compile that schema into Strategus R code for an OMOP environment. Across eight models and 30 studies, primary-analysis standardization accuracy ranged from 0.93 to 0.97 for OHDSI studies and 0.82 to 0.95 outside OHDSI.

Those numbers cover four core specification groups, not a complete study. Unsupported analytic choices were excluded, cohort definition remains separate, and the researchers did not test whether THESEUS saves users time or improves end-to-end validity. Its value is the constraint itself: turn protocol interpretation into structured extraction, make each field reviewable, and reserve code generation for a deterministic compiler.

Anonymisation is becoming a recipient-and-attack test

The European Data Protection Board opened consultation on Guidelines 02/2026 on July 8. The draft tests anonymity through three criteria: No Record Isolation, No Linkage, and No Inference. Its contextual route asks what a relevant recipient or other entity can reasonably do with the data and additional information; a simplified route deliberately overestimates risk when that context is too uncertain.

The draft is European GDPR guidance, not HIPAA Expert Determination and not a final rule. Still, the methodological rhyme is strong: anonymity is not a property conferred by deleting a list of fields. It depends on the data, the recipient, available linkage and inference means, and the release environment. The guidance also calls for documentation and periodic reassessment and says a security incident can change the result.

A separate engineering study shows why technique-level claims need their own limits. D. Vallejo-Sanchez and colleagues compared Spanish clinical-text de-identification strategies on a synthetic benchmark and real obstetric notes. As-is task models and locally run prompt-based LLMs reached macro F1 scores from 0.14 to 0.50. Fine-tuned task models reached as high as 0.956.

The generic LLMs also made small changes to surrounding text, disrupting token alignment and information preservation. Fine-tuning is the clear engineering winner in this study, but F1 is not a re-identification-risk assessment. A model can remove named entities accurately without establishing that the remaining text is safe for a particular recipient, linkage environment, or intended use.

What changed: validation is moving upstream

The genuinely new signal is a set of concrete failure tests. Exposure definitions can reverse a result after the balance table turns green. Deep learning can fail alongside conventional adjustment when confounding is absent from the data. Prompt-only causal analysis can sound polished while bearing no relationship to ground truth.

The constructive response is also becoming clearer. Constrain agents to schemas and deterministic tools. Make operationalization sensitivity and negative controls routine. Preserve intermediate artifacts. Require the workflow to state when overlap changes the estimand or when a result should not be certified.

The recurring theme is that neither AI nor privacy technology makes evidence self-validating. Better machinery can make the scientific and risk decisions easier to inspect. It cannot make those decisions disappear.
