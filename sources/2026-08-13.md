## Ranked evidence

### 1. FDA selected the first AI-enabled participant for an RWE-generating digital-health pilot

- Direct source: [FDA participant announcement](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-announces-first-participant-selected-tempo-digital-health-devices-pilot)
- Supporting primary sources: [FDA TEMPO program page](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/digital-health-center-excellence/tempo-digital-health-devices-pilot), [CMS ACCESS model](https://www.cms.gov/priorities/innovation/innovation-models/access), [Dexcom participant announcement](https://investors.dexcom.com/news/news-details/2026/Dexcom-Announced-as-First-Participant-Selected-for-FDAs-TEMPO-Digital-Health-Devices-Pilot-Program/default.aspx)
- Publication date: July 22, 2026
- Stable ID: `url:https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-announces-first-participant-selected-tempo-digital-health-devices-pilot`
- Content version: `first-participant-announcement`
- Internal score: 9/10
- `ai_rwe_intersection`: **passes**. The selected program uses AI to integrate glucose and contextual health data and provide personalized insights; TEMPO requires participating manufacturers to collect, monitor, and report RWD tied to the device's intended uses.
- What the primary sources establish:
  - FDA selected Dexcom as the first participant in the Technology-Enabled Meaningful Patient Outcomes (TEMPO) pilot.
  - The Dexcom Glucose Health Program is intended to support two clinical-use areas in CMS's ACCESS model: screening for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes, and potential improvement of glycemic control and HbA1c in people with prediabetes.
  - FDA describes the program as offering real-time data and AI insights. Dexcom says it will integrate data from G7 and Stelo glucose sensors with nutrition, physical activity, sleep, and stress to provide personalized insights to users and clinicians.
  - Participating manufacturers must collect, monitor, and report RWD relating to the intended uses of their devices. FDA says selection considerations include risk, expected benefit, the RWD collection and analysis plan, and alignment with ACCESS outcomes.
  - FDA plans to select up to about ten participants in each of four ACCESS clinical-use areas.
  - CMS's ACCESS model began July 5, 2026 and uses outcome-aligned payments for technology-supported chronic care. The model is intended to operate for ten years.
- Why it matters: this is a concrete attempt to make real-world evidence part of the access pathway for an AI-enabled health program rather than a retrospective add-on. The arrangement can reveal whether AI-supported recommendations translate into measurable outcomes under routine use, but only if the evidence plan is sufficiently transparent and rigorous.
- Commercial context: Dexcom's description of the AI architecture and intended benefits comes from its investor-relations announcement. It is useful product detail, not independent validation.
- Limits: neither the FDA nor Dexcom announcement publishes a participant-level protocol, sample size, comparator, analysis plan, monitoring cadence, subgroup plan, algorithm-change rules, or decision thresholds. No performance or outcome result has been reported. The broader TEMPO framework can allow requests for enforcement discretion for some requirements, but the July 22 announcement does not specify which requirements, if any, apply to this participant.
- Follow-up: watch for the participant protocol, outcome definitions, cohort and comparator design, data-quality and missingness plan, subgroup reporting, model-change documentation, adverse-event monitoring, and the evidence threshold FDA and CMS will use for continuation, coverage, or authorization.

## What changed

The AI-plus-RWE intersection is moving from retrospective validation studies toward prospective regulatory and payment infrastructure. TEMPO and ACCESS make evidence generation part of the operating bargain: an AI-enabled program gains a path into routine chronic-care delivery while its manufacturer generates RWD for regulators and payers. The unresolved issue is whether the public evidence design will be detailed enough to distinguish product learning from regulatory-grade inference.
