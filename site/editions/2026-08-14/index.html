The Agents Screened 5,598 Papers. Their Top First Pass Missed 43% of Eligible Studies.

MacAma put retrieval, screening, analysis, and writing inside one multi-agent workflow—then showed why speed is not the same thing as evidence control

August 14, 2026 — Friday edition

The new multi-agent system did not merely help write a systematic review. It attempted the review from search box to manuscript.

MacAma, published July 29 in Smart Medicine, connects literature retrieval, title-and-abstract screening, full-text review, effect-size analysis, plotting, and report generation. The authors also released an open repository with code, workflow resources, benchmark data, and a complete case package.

In a review of radiotherapy and anti-tumor immunity, the system started with 5,598 records. The authors estimate that work conventionally requiring more than a year and a half was completed in under four days. But the agent configuration with the strongest initial-screening F1 recalled only 56.55% of eligible records in its held-out benchmark.

That is the useful result. Agentic evidence synthesis can now reach across the workflow. Its weakest checkpoint can still decide which evidence ceases to exist downstream.

The first screen was precise. It was not complete.

The title-and-abstract benchmark contained 354 manually labeled records that were kept separate from prompt development. The DeepSeek-R1 workflow achieved the best F1 in the table, 0.7069. It was highly precise—94.25% of the records it called eligible were eligible—but its recall was 56.55%.

Put differently, it missed 43.45% of the eligible positives in that benchmark. Qwen-max produced nearly the same F1 with 59.31% recall. A conventional SciBERT classifier had lower precision but recovered 71% of eligible records.

Systematic reviews usually protect recall because an excluded study cannot contribute an effect estimate, change a subgroup finding, or expose heterogeneity. A highly precise agent may save reviewers time while quietly narrowing the evidence base. That is not necessarily the wrong tradeoff, but it must be chosen explicitly and tested against the conclusion of the review.

The extraordinary number came from a narrower second task

MacAma's full-text benchmark was much stronger: 98.83% accuracy, 100% precision, 97.49% recall, and an F1 of 0.9873 across 599 records. Removing the structured extraction stage or compressing the screening instructions materially reduced performance.

Those figures do not erase the first-screen result. The benchmarks measure different decisions on different sets. The full-text test included 599 of the 749 obtainable articles for which complete text and human labels were available. It therefore says that the protocol-constrained system performed exceptionally well on that bounded full-text task, not that 98.83% accuracy governed every stage of the review.

The authors designed that distinction into the system. Lower-risk repetitive tasks can be delegated; chart extraction, statistical choices, and interpretive steps are verified by experts. Screening rationales are retained to make decisions inspectable. The repository makes the workflow more reproducible than a product demonstration, although running it still requires external model services, configured application IDs, and independent validation in a new domain.

The speed claims should receive the same discipline. The paper estimates about 56 hours for initial screening instead of nine to twelve months, roughly 24 hours for full-text work instead of six to eight months, and under four days end to end instead of more than 18 months. Those are author-supplied case-study estimates, not a controlled time-and-motion comparison. The independent question is whether the same conclusion survives the time saved.

Friday synthesis: the agents got broader; the evidence controls did not get optional

This week's work expanded the territory of AI-assisted evidence. Agents built cohorts, reconciled contradictory trial records, helped assess safety signals, extracted cancer staging at scale, entered a prospective regulatory evidence program, and now carried a meta-analysis across five connected stages.

CoverageA workflow cannot recover the study, patient fact, or safety event that its retrieval or source record never exposes.
ValidationStrong performance on one bounded label does not validate the next transformation or the final evidence claim.
AccountabilityHuman review is moving toward error thresholds, adjudication, and inference rather than disappearing from the pipeline.

Wednesday's review evidence showed an LLM-assisted search recovering only 51% of the final evidence set even as later screening saved time. Thursday's regulatory evidence showed FDA and CMS coupling an AI program to prospective RWD collection before publishing the participant-level protocol. MacAma joins those signals at the research-workflow layer: unprecedented reach, visible checkpoints, and a missingness problem that can still outrun the agent.

The contradiction is the frontier. The greatest efficiency gains arrive where omissions are hardest to notice. The next generation of evidence agents should be judged on missed studies, changed effect estimates, disagreement with reviewers, and whether conclusions remain stable—not only on accuracy, throughput, or a manuscript produced on schedule.
